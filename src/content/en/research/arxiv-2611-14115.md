Progress in spatial understanding and embodied reasoning has made it attractive to let foundation models drive robots directly. A planner can decompose a long-horizon task in natural language, re-sequence steps when something goes wrong, and dispatch actions through tool calls. The more capable the system, the higher the price of a mistake: a decision to pour cleaner into a coffee machine, or to swing an arm fast next to a human hand, does not become harmless because a language model produced it.

The starting observation is an awkward one. Safety evaluation for such systems is thin. Existing benchmarks cover either whether a task should be attempted at all, or whether an action collides during execution, and several score text outputs without ever closing the loop. Real deployment risk is not partitioned that way. A model may refuse a perfectly legitimate task on step one, and it may repeat an irreversible operation on step seven because its memory of the sequence has drifted.

### Core innovation

The first contribution is the RoboRisk benchmark, which splits risk into five categories: task-level refusal, whether the model recognises instructions that should not be executed or are ambiguous; plan repair, whether it can replan when the initial plan is infeasible; execution-time hazard, whether the action itself is safe in its physical context; injection, whether hostile text or imagery in the environment can hijack behaviour; and sequential memory, whether state is carried correctly across a multi-step task. All 92 simulated and 13 physical tasks run in closed loop, which is the essential difference from text evaluation: the model must actually emit the action and live with the consequence.

The second contribution is the breadth of the sweep. Six frontier foundation models are compared on one task suite, alongside two robot-specific APIs. The finding is unflattering: harm rates are generally high. That result carries information by itself, because it says the problem is not a single model's alignment gap but a property of the architecture that lets a language model act as the robot's policy.

The third is LEASH, a safety framework that can be integrated into almost any agentic architecture. Its structure is the interesting part. A decision model implements three gates: a task gate reviews the whole task, an action gate reviews each action, and a commit gate reviews each irreversible operation, all in visual context. Planner and gates share one safety policy, which avoids the split-brain case where the planner believes an action is fine and the gate disagrees. In experiments LEASH reduces harm across all six models, largely preserves task completion, and adds little time or token overhead.

### Results

The headline quantitative claim is that six frontier models show generally high harm rates on RoboRisk, while LEASH consistently lowers harm in simulation and on physical robots with limited damage to task capability and modest additional time and token cost. The abstract does not report per-model harm rates, per-category breakdowns, or the end-to-end latency the gates introduce.

The evidence structure deserves credit for not stopping at diagnosis. Safety work often stops once a benchmark has exposed a problem; here the mitigation is validated on the same tasks, and the paper explicitly reports that task completion does not collapse. That trade-off is precisely what industry evaluates when deciding whether to adopt a safety component.

### Limitations

All three gates depend on a decision model, which outsources part of the safety guarantee to another learned system that can itself fail. The paper does not report the gates' own miss and false-alarm rates, nor whether a gate sharing the planner's visual misperceptions produces correlated rather than independent failures. Thirteen physical tasks is a small sample, and the five categories may omit multi-robot coordination and long-run drift. Latency is described only as small, with no p95 figures, even though the gates sit on the critical path of a real-time control loop. The tasks are author-constructed, so coverage of real production risk distributions still needs external validation.

### Industry implications

The most direct commercial value is turning safety from a principle into a measurable score. Teams building agentic humanoids or mobile manipulators can borrow the five-category split as an internal red-team checklist, especially injection and sequential memory, which almost never appear in demos and are among the most likely causes of a customer-site incident.

The gate architecture also offers a deployable pattern: isolate irreversible operations and re-check them once against visual context before execution. That commit gate echoes transaction commit in databases and interlocks in industrial control, suggesting robot safety is moving from ad hoc collision checking toward layered, auditable authorisation. The real dividing line will be liability. Once logs can show that an action passed three gates and still caused harm, whether responsibility sits with the model vendor, the integrator or the deployer will decide if frameworks like this become an industry standard or an optional plugin.
