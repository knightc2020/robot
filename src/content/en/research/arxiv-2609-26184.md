Putting a large language model into a robot's control loop has been the clearest paradigm shift of the past two years: planning, task decomposition, and judgment under ambiguous instructions — things that once demanded bespoke engineering — can now be borrowed from a general model. The price is a new component in the control loop whose behavior is difficult to fully explain. Security research has long fixed its attention on the output side, asking whether the model will propose a dangerous action. This paper looks further inward: if the danger comes not from what the robot saw but from what it has already done, where exactly should the defenses sit?

### Background

Existing backdoor research on LLMs and embodied agents places the trigger in externally observable channels: a specific word in text, a specific object in view, scene semantics, or an explicit action sequence. All of these share a weakness that also gives defenders a handle — they are external, and therefore can be filtered, detected, or masked. The paper points out a second class of trigger conditions that has been systematically overlooked: conditions that originate not in the environment but in the agent's own operating history.

### Core Innovation

The paper delivers the first systematic study of **history-based backdoor attacks** on LLM-powered robotic systems. The construction works as follows:

- The attacker **manipulates the robot's instructions** to embed a backdoor in an LLM-based controller, without touching hardware or sensors.
- The trigger is not an external stimulus but a **rare sequence of the robot's own past actions** — a specific pattern in the internal state trajectory.
- The backdoor remains **dormant** during normal operation, leaving task utility intact; once that sequence occurs it activates and induces malicious behavior such as a **complete stop** or a **collision**.

The crux is that it relocates the attack surface from the world to the state: a defender who fully validates inputs, filters language, and watches vision may still never inspect the agent's own action history.

### Results

- Simulated experiments covering **multiple robots** and **multiple LLM** controllers.
- The history-based attack reaches a **near-perfect attack success rate**.
- It is simultaneously **exceptionally hard to detect**: the backdoor stays dormant in normal operation and produces no observable performance degradation.

### Limitations

All results are simulated, with no real-robot validation, so it is unclear how real sensor noise, execution error, and communication latency would affect the precise reproduction of a rare action sequence — the more a trigger depends on an exact historical pattern, the more easily reality can interrupt it. The abstract omits the model list, the robot platforms, the concrete success-rate figures, and how the rare sequence is searched for or constructed, which is what would determine the feasibility bar for an attacker. Defenses are proposed but not evaluated: the paper argues that agent internal state must enter the security perimeter, without offering a usable detection or mitigation. It also assumes an attacker able to manipulate instructions, which maps onto familiar supply-chain or fine-tuning-poisoning scenarios, but the required level of access is not discussed.

### Industry Implications

For deployers, this paper redraws the boundary of defense. Current robot-security practice looks inward at model outputs and outward at environmental inputs, and a history-based trigger sits precisely in the gap — it is neither a malicious input nor an anomalous output. That has direct consequences. First, logging and observability must be upgraded: if the trigger is a rare pattern in action history, retaining and analyzing state trajectories turns from a debugging convenience into safety infrastructure. Second, model provenance governance matters more: since a backdoor can be planted by manipulating instructions, the supply chain for the controller's model and instructions needs auditing like any software dependency. Third, evaluation criteria need a new category — not just task success in nominal operation and robustness under adversarial inputs, but behavioral consistency under specific internal states. None of these are answers the paper provides; they are the questions it forces the field to start asking.
