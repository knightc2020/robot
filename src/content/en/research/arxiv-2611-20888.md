Humanoid control has converged on two recipes, and each buys something at the cost of something else. Adversarial motion priors give a policy style: the discriminator pushes generated motion toward the distribution of human demonstrations, so the robot moves like a person rather than like an optimiser. The problem is deployment — the resulting policies are notoriously fragile on hardware. Whole-body tracking through reinforcement learning solves deployment instead: train the policy to follow reference joint trajectories, and it reproduces demonstrations faithfully and runs reliably on a real robot.

Tracking has a structural catch. The policy is conditioned on the future reference, so it needs that reference at runtime, and when the commanded motion drifts outside the training distribution the policy has nothing valid to condition on. The robot does not degrade gracefully; it fails.

### Core innovation

The authors keep the tracking reward and remove the reference from the actor's input. The actor sees only compact locomotion commands — velocity — and robot observations. The reference motion instead supervises two things that are not executed at deployment: the reward signal, and a privileged critic used during training.

This is a small change with a large consequence. Style is no longer something the policy reads off a trajectory; it is something baked into the policy by the reward, so the robot reproduces the whole-body gait when commanded to move at a velocity, with no trajectory to follow and no teacher to distil from. The paper is explicit that no teacher distillation is used, which matters because distillation is the usual escape hatch and it inherits the teacher's dependence on references.

### Results

The evaluation is deliberately adversarial to the method. Training uses a single random-walk motion clip. Testing commands the policy with velocity profiles taken from held-out motions — walking patterns it never saw and was never rewarded on.

In simulation the policy reaches at least 98% success. On a Unitree G1 it completes every trial.

The comparison is where the argument lands. On held-out slow walking, whole-body tracking drops to 2% success and command-conditioned distillation to 56%, and both fail on the real robot. That is the sharpest evidence in the paper: two strong baselines that are fine on their training distribution become unusable under a modest distribution shift, while a policy trained with the same reward but a narrower input space keeps working.

On the motions it was trained on, the policy's joint position error stays within 8% of whole-body tracking, so the input restriction does not cost fidelity where fidelity is measurable. The authors also report higher style coverage than adversarial motion priors, and note that the AMP baseline fell on the real robot without a safety crane — a detail that reframes the whole comparison, since a policy that needs a crane is not deployable.

### Limitations

The method is trained on one motion — a random walk — and its style coverage is measured on trained motions. That is a narrow repertoire, and the paper does not show how many distinct motion clips the approach can absorb before the velocity-conditioned interface becomes the limiting factor. A velocity command has very little bandwidth; it can specify how fast and which direction, but not how to move an arm or where to place a foot.

Because the policy no longer follows a reference, there is also no mechanism for precise motion reproduction when a task needs a specific pose rather than a specific style. That is an inherent trade: the paper buys generalisation and deployability by giving up exact trajectory following.

All hardware evidence is on a single humanoid platform, and the tasks are locomotion, not manipulation or contact. The interaction between this command-conditioned style and a learned upper-body manipulation policy is untested.

### Industry implications

The practical value here is reliability under command variation, which is the condition commercial humanoid deployments actually face. A robot in a warehouse or a plant is not replayed a motion capture clip; it is told to move somewhere at some speed, and the terrain, the payload, and the operator's instructions all shift. A policy that only works when the command matches its training reference is a demo, not a product.

The result also suggests a cheaper path to stylised motion. If the style can be transferred through the reward alone, teams do not need runtime motion libraries, retargeting pipelines, or a reference-trajectory generator in the deployed stack. That simplifies the runtime considerably and removes a component that has to be maintained per customer site.

For the research community, the honest reading is that the standard whole-body tracking benchmark flatters tracking policies. Reporting success on the training motions says little; the held-out-velocity protocol used here is a better test, and it is cheap to adopt.
