The hard part of a dexterous hand is rarely the mechanism alone. Given a manipulation requirement, a team designs link geometry, writes the robot description, tunes collision bodies, fixes grasp initialisation, then trains a policy and watches it fail. A failure at any of those stages surfaces as an untrainable policy, while the actual cause may sit in geometry, collision meshes or initial conditions. This paper treats that trial-and-error loop itself as the problem to be solved.

Task2Hand is presented as a Skill: a specification package that takes a functional manipulation brief as input, emits a parameterised hand model as output, and routes every candidate through three gates.

### Core innovation

The gates are ordered by cost — contact, dynamics, then learning — and that ordering is the design's core. Contact and dynamics resolve in seconds to minutes; the learning gate requires a full training run. Cheap gates eliminate obviously poor geometry so that training budget is spent on few candidates.

Each gate returns a status plus an evidence record. On failure the system notes the earliest blocker, revises one bounded parameter group, and reruns the cheaper gates. This restriction matters: if a single failure licensed edits to several parameter groups at once, coupling between them would invalidate the evidence and iteration would degrade into random search.

Within declared bounds the agent generates, validates and trains autonomously. On trade-offs, performance plateaus or conflicting evidence it escalates to a human who sets the next design direction. The division of labour moves people from tuning to steering.

### Results

The evidence-driven trajectory produced SAGE Hand: four fingers, 16 actuated degrees of freedom, an integrated guiding palm, compact roots, cylindrical tips, and a collision-cleared opposable thumb. Each feature maps to a concrete failure mode — the guiding palm stabilises the object within the hand, cylindrical tips improve rolling contact, and the collision-cleared thumb determines whether opposition can actually close.

Under a matched training protocol, SAGE Hand reaches 45.6% task success on a hexagonal prism against 37.3% for the LEAP hand, and 70.8% on a bicolor cylinder against 51.6%. The matched protocol is what makes the comparison meaningful; otherwise geometry and training budget would be confounded.

Two further results stand on their own. Dense simulated tactile input more than halves the cylinder drop rate, showing that sensing modality and mechanism design are coupled here. In a controlled iteration study the Skill-guided path preserved capture in 164 of 165 trials while the path without the Skill degraded to 14 — the gates function as a regression guard during refinement, not merely an initial screen.

Accepted candidates ship with URDF/MJCF, metrics, checkpoints and a provenance manifest, which is what makes the work reproducible.

### Limitations

Every result is simulated. No hardware experiment appears in the abstract, yet dexterous manipulation's hardest problems live in real contact: fingertip friction, soft contact, and the hysteresis that cable or tendon routing introduces are all hard to reproduce in simulation. The 45.6% absolute success rate also confirms that bidirectional in-hand manipulation is far from solved; even the winning design fails more than half its trials.

The tactile conclusion rests on simulated touch. Real tactile sensors differ substantially in noise characteristics and coverage, and whether dense touch would still halve the drop rate on hardware is not answered.

The design space is bounded by what a human declares in advance, so the method's ceiling is set by that declaration. The authors also concede that plateaus and conflicting evidence require human intervention — autonomy is not unbounded.

### Industry implications

For teams building dexterous hands, the transferable artifact is a process rather than a hand. Making geometry, collision and trainability checks into cost-ordered gates with evidence records cuts wasted training and turns design decisions from individual judgement into an auditable trail.

The packaging convention is the second lesson. URDF/MJCF plus metrics, checkpoints and a provenance manifest means a hand design can be reproduced, compared and handed over by downstream teams — worth more to supply-chain collaboration than the headline numbers.

One caveat should be held. Sixteen actuated DoF is a real burden on cost, weight and reliability. An extra degree of freedom in simulation is one more action dimension; on hardware it is another motor, another cable run and another failure point. Whether SAGE Hand's gains justify that complexity needs hardware cost data to settle.
