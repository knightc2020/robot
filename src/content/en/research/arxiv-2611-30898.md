The largest source of manipulation experience is not robot data. It is video: egocentric footage of people doing household work, plus heterogeneous recordings from many robot fleets. Both are rich in dynamics and poor in exactly the label supervised policy learning wants. Human video has no actions at all, and robot recordings from different embodiments do not share an action space. Latent action models were invented to bridge that gap, but most of them train a fresh inverse-dynamics model and a fresh forward-dynamics model, and spend their capacity trading pixel reconstruction against latent accuracy.

This paper asks a different question. A pretrained video world model already generates high-fidelity dynamics; that knowledge is exactly what a latent action model is trying to learn. So why train one from scratch? The obstacle is that the world model represents dynamics densely and entangled across all its layers, with no explicit handle for "the action that caused this change."

### Core innovation

Native LAM makes the world model its own action model with minimal structural change. A single pretrained video diffusion transformer plays both roles: the inverse-dynamics model that infers what happened between two frames, and the forward-dynamics model that predicts the next frame. The only new causal channel is a set of learnable dynamics registers. They sit between the two roles and are the sole path by which inferred motion reaches the generative side, forcing the registers to carry a compact summary of the transition.

Training uses counterfactual temporal pairs chosen to differ in motion alone, so the registers cannot cheat by encoding appearance or scene identity. What they distill is a native latent action: a compact code for how the scene moved, extracted from a model that already knows how scenes move.

The second half of the paper, PAWlicy, turns this into a pretraining recipe for vision-language-action policies. Native LAM acts as a dynamics teacher over large-scale video, and the student VLA learns to predict the teacher's latent actions from its own observations and instructions. Crucially, unlike world-action models, the teacher never generates video at deployment; it only supplies targets during pretraining.

### Results

On the LARY benchmark, native latent actions outperform existing latent action models on both action classification and control regression. More interesting is what happens without any transfer objective: the same latent action replays the same motion on new robots, in new scenes, and from human hands to robots. That zero-shot replay across embodiments is the property practitioners actually need, since it means the representation is grounded in motion rather than in a particular arm.

As a pretraining stage, PAWlicy with a 3.8B model reaches a 93.6% average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 when trained with domain-randomized demonstrations, which the authors report as the highest among VLAs. On RoboDojo it is competitive on every measured dimension, with precision on par with leading leaderboard entries. On real robots it improves over the pi_0.5 baseline on both tasks tested.

### Limitations

The headline 93.6% comes from RoboTwin 2.0 with domain-randomized demonstrations, a setting that rewards exactly the kind of broad motion prior this method builds; whether the margin holds under narrow, real-world data budgets is less clear. The real-robot evidence is two tasks, described qualitatively as improvements over pi_0.5, without the scale of the simulated suite.

The approach also depends on the quality of the underlying public world model. If that model's dynamics are biased toward the video distribution it was trained on, the registers inherit the bias, and the abstract does not analyse failure modes on contact-rich or highly deformable interaction. Compute cost of the two-stage pipeline is not reported either, which matters for groups deciding whether to adopt it.

### Industry implications

The strategic point is that a public video world model can be converted into a pretraining asset for robot policies without ever running video generation in the control loop. That sidesteps the latency objection that has kept world-action models out of real-time deployment, and it lets teams with video but little robot data bootstrap a policy.

The cross-embodiment replay result is the more quietly important one. A latent action that transfers from human hands to a robot, with no explicit transfer loss, is the kind of interface that reduces the cost of adapting a policy to a new arm. If it holds beyond the demonstrated settings, the practical workflow becomes: pretrain dynamics on whatever video is available, then fine-tune lightly per embodiment, rather than collect action-labelled data for every robot separately.
