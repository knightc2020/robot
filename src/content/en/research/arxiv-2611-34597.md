Model predictive control keeps winning arguments it cannot win at runtime. The reason is structural: at every tick it re-solves a constrained optimization over a horizon, and the moment the model becomes expressive — a learned Koopman lifting into hundreds or thousands of dimensions — the decision vector grows with the lifted state and the solver stops fitting the control loop, let alone a microcontroller.

μMPC attacks the accounting rather than the solver. Its observation is that the lifted state within a horizon is a deterministic function of the control inputs, so the entire lifted trajectory can be precomputed as a control-to-output map. Substituting that map into the optimization eliminates the lifted states as decision variables and leaves what the authors call a Koopman-Compiled QP: the only variables are the H·n_u inputs, a count that no longer depends on how large the lifted space is. Building that map offline is the trade; at runtime the problem is a small QP.

The second ingredient makes that small QP cheap to iterate. A fixed, precomputed primal operator supports primal-dual iterations that need only matrix-vector products and projections, so a changing goal or an activated constraint does not force a refactorization — the expensive step that normally dominates warm-started solvers in embedded settings. Because the whole thing is differentiable, the same machinery can be dropped into the training loop, letting the dynamics model be learned with control performance in mind and letting batched DAgger-style data aggregation run cheaply.

The reported latencies span an unusually wide range of hardware and problem sizes: 23 μs on a desktop CPU, 24 ms on a 168 MHz Crazyflie microcontroller, 4096-dimensional quadruped control in under 5 ms, visual planning against a 2048-dimensional world model in 0.4 ms, and collision-aware planning for 200 quadrotors at more than 30 Hz on a GPU. The headline multipliers — 108× faster constrained MPC, 1907× faster visuomotor planning — are against competing methods on these benchmarks, and they are the kind of figure that changes what is architecturally possible rather than merely what is affordable: MPC at kilohertz rates on an MCU, or world-model-based planning fast enough to sit inside a reactive loop.

What the abstract does not settle is where the approximation bites. Koopman-linearized dynamics are only as trustworthy as the lifting, and a constrained QP solved fast returns the wrong answer quickly if the model mispredicts near contact, saturation or a hard nonlinearity — precisely the regimes where constrained MPC is usually deployed. The precomputation also front-loads cost and memory into the horizon and input dimensions, so the very scaling that the method buys at runtime has to be paid somewhere offline; the paper's benchmarks are its own, and the claim of control-aware learning is demonstrated as a capability, not as an accuracy result against established model-learning baselines. Safety guarantees, if any, inherit the fidelity of the learned model.

Still, for the part of the stack that everyone treats as fixed overhead, this is a meaningful shift. Real-time control cost is often the reason a simpler controller gets shipped, a lower control rate gets accepted, or visual world models stay in the offline evaluation harness. Cutting the constrained solve to microseconds on a CPU and milliseconds on an MCU moves those decisions, and does it with an optimization formulation rather than an approximation that has to be re-validated from scratch.
