Robot learning has spent two years split between two camps. One builds vision-language-action policies, fine-tuned on teleoperated demonstrations, that map pixels to motor commands. The other wraps a frontier foundation model in an agent harness and lets it call skills as tools. The first camp owns benchmark scores but needs expensive data for every embodiment; the second camp gets open-world flexibility but inherits the latency and fragility of a multi-stage pipeline.

This paper argues that a third route is now practical: take an open-weight reasoning model and post-train it with reinforcement learning in simulation until it controls a robot natively. No demonstrations, no harness, no separate skill library.

### Core innovation

The authors build PEAR, a post-training engine for agentic robotics, and train with a procedure they call robot-use reinforcement learning. The model is given the same interface a computer-use agent gets, except the action space is the next end-effector pose rather than a mouse click. It observes the environment, writes a chain of thought, and emits the pose. That is the entire loop.

Two design choices carry the result. First, training happens at scale in simulation on LIBERO and RoboDojo, so the supervision is reward rather than human teleoperation. Second, the reasoning is kept in the loop at training time, which is what allows the model to transfer to novel instructions: the policy is not memorising a trajectory, it is learning to decompose a task it has been told about.

The claim that matters is generalisation. The authors report transfer to an unseen robot, to the real world, and to instructions outside the training distribution — three different gaps, each of which normally breaks a demonstration-trained policy.

### Results

On a real dual-arm robot, after training only in simulation, the model succeeds in 60% of trials; the base model succeeds in none. On LIBERO the success rate rises from 1.6% to 74.4%, which the authors describe as approaching GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol when those models are run at maximum reasoning effort.

The LIBERO-Pro result is the sharper one. When the same scene is reused but the task is new, the demonstration-tuned VLA π0.5 succeeds in 0.75% of episodes while this model succeeds in 57.4%. A benchmark gain can always be attributed to fitting the benchmark; a seventy-fold gap under task reassignment cannot, because the policy has to read the instruction and re-plan.

The authors also check that general capability survived post-training, reporting no measurable degradation across nine reasoning, coding and vision benchmarks. That is a necessary control: a model that gets good at robots by destroying its reasoning would be a poor foundation model, and the paper is explicitly positioned as post-training rather than replacement.

### Limitations

All training is in simulation, and the real-world evidence is 60% success on a single dual-arm platform. Sixty percent is a strong result for a zero-demonstration method, but it also means two of every five trials fail, and the abstract does not characterise those failures.

The action interface is a next end-effector pose. That is a deliberately narrow claim: it shows high-level visuomotor competence, not whole-body control, dexterous in-hand manipulation, or force-aware contact. Whatever converts the pose into joint commands is outside the learned loop.

The paper also does not report inference latency or achievable control frequency, which is the central deployment question for a chain-of-thought controller. Reasoning tokens cost time, and a policy that needs a long deliberation before each pose is a different product from one that reacts at servo rate. Finally, reward design in simulation remains the hidden labour here; the method removes demonstrations but not the task engineering that produces the reward.

### Industry implications

The commercial signal is that the data moat around robot learning may be shallower than assumed. If a 27B open-weight model can be post-trained on simulated tasks and then run on hardware it never saw, the expensive part of the pipeline shifts from demonstration collection to simulation fidelity and reward design — both of which are software assets that compound across embodiments.

It also changes the competitive position of open models. The abstract frames frontier proprietary systems as the only ones with strong robot-use performance; a result like this closes that gap with weights anyone can download and fine-tune, which is exactly the condition under which robotics companies stop treating the foundation model as a vendor dependency.

The open question is reliability engineering. A reasoning controller that fails 40% of the time is a research result; a product needs failure detection, recovery and a bounded worst case. The natural next step is the one this paper does not take: pairing the reasoning policy with a verifier or a low-level safety layer that can reject an action before it is executed.
