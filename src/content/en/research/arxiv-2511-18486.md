In magnetically guided surgery, the fantasy is a magnetic field that can steer a tool anywhere inside the body. The reality is a coil array with a power budget. Push enough current through the coils to control a tool at a distance and the windings heat up; cap the temperature and the usable workspace shrinks to whatever geometry the remaining current can reach. This paper's argument is that a surprising amount of that shrinkage was self-inflicted, and that better control design buys back workspace without buying new hardware.

### Background

Electromagnetic navigation systems (eMNS) steer magnetically responsive tools for surgical procedures, and their appeal is minimal invasiveness: no mechanical shaft has to reach the target. But the effective workspace of an eMNS — the region in which a tool can be posed with sufficient force and torque — is set by power and thermal limits rather than by the coils' nominal reach. The conventional strategy is field-centric: drive the coils so the magnetic field at the tool points the way the tool should point, which is intuitive but draws currents far larger than the motion actually demands. Because that current is what heats the hardware, a control strategy that wastes it is directly spending workspace.

### Core Innovation

The paper identifies five system-level ingredients that together expand the workspace:

- **Motion-centric torque and force objectives.** Optimise the magnetic wrench the tool actually needs to move, rather than the field direction that makes the control law look natural.
- **Energy-optimal current allocation.** Among the many coil-current combinations that produce a given wrench, choose the one that minimises power.
- **Real-time pose estimation.** Close the loop around the tool's measured state so the controller can act on error instead of on a precomputed plan.
- **Dynamic feedback.** Treat the tool-plus-system as a dynamical system to be stabilised, not as a static pointing problem.
- **High-bandwidth eMNS components.** Give the controller enough speed for feedback to matter.

The proof of concept is a 3D inverted pendulum balanced on an eight-coil OctoMag platform. Swapping field-centric alignment for motion-centric torque and force control stabilises the pendulum at **0.1–0.2 A instead of 8–14 A**. The authors then generalise to multi-agent manipulation, exploiting magnetic-field nonlinearity and coil redundancy to stabilise two inverted pendulums independently inside one shared workspace, and provide a structured comparison of the electromagnetic workspaces achievable under the two control paradigms.

### Results

- **Roughly two orders of magnitude less current.** Stable 3D inverted-pendulum balancing at 0.1–0.2 A versus 8–14 A for the field-centric baseline.
- **Multi-agent control in a shared workspace.** Two inverted pendulums stabilised simultaneously, deriving independent actuation from field nonlinearity and coil redundancy.
- **Cross-platform clinical relevance.** On the clinically oriented Navion eMNS, stable balancing is maintained at distances up to **50 cm from the coils** — a substantial workspace expansion.
- **A workspace analysis** comparing the electromagnetic reach of the two paradigms and mapping motion objectives onto coil currents.

### Limitations

The benchmark is an inverted pendulum, not a surgical procedure, so the leap from stabilising a pendulum to navigating a catheter or a capsule through tissue is inferred rather than shown. The multi-agent demonstration stops at two agents in a controlled setup, and how the independent-actuation trick scales with more agents and tighter packing is not established. Thermal limits are the motivation for the whole argument, yet the abstract reports no temperature measurements, duty-cycle analysis, or cooldown model; the current reduction is a proxy for reduced heating, not a measurement of it. Pose estimation and high-bandwidth components are named as requirements but their accuracy and latency are not quantified, and the loop is closed on rigid-body balancing rather than on compliant tissue contact. Finally, the abstract gives no in-vivo or ex-vivo validation, so safety and efficacy in a biological setting remain future work.

### Industry Implications

For surgical robotics, workspace is capability: every centimetre of reachable anatomy is another procedure that a magnetically steered tool can attempt rather than one that needs a rigid shaft. A two-orders-of-magnitude reduction in actuation current is therefore not just an efficiency result — it is a route to reaching deeper targets through the same coil hardware, or to shrinking the coil array needed for a given target. The second implication is safety. Heating is a primary hazard in magnetic navigation, so a control law that accomplishes a motion with a fraction of the current has a direct patient-safety argument attached to it, independent of the workspace gain. Multi-agent control opens a further door: two tools in one working volume, or one tool plus an independent magnetic marker, using the same array. The gap to close is clinical — demonstrating the current reduction on a real instrument in real tissue, with temperature measurements and in-vivo safety evidence, is what would turn a compelling control result into a deployable one.
