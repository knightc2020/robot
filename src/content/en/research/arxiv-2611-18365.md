Automating a car is a solved-enough problem that the remaining work is mostly perception and validation. Automating a wheel loader is not, for two reasons that have nothing to do with software fashion. The dynamics are genuinely hostile — hydraulic actuation involves fluid compressibility, flexible elements and nonlinear delays, so the machine's response to a command is not a clean function of its state. And the machine weighs five tonnes, which makes the experimental loop unforgiving: a policy that is wrong in an interesting way does not produce a bad episode, it produces a wreck.

The usual response is to automate the boring parts and leave dynamic operation to humans. This paper goes the other way and automates the part humans only do in record attempts.

### Core innovation

The approach is reinforcement learning in simulation followed by sim-to-real transfer, with the target being the machine's full dynamic envelope rather than a nominal driving task. The platform is a John Deere 332G, an operational 5-tonne skid-steer loader — not a scaled testbed.

What makes skid-steer acrobatics a useful stress test is that the manoeuvre is only possible near the limits of actuation. Getting a loader onto two wheels requires committing momentum in a way that has no safe partial version; the policy must model the transition into and out of the unstable equilibrium rather than avoid it. Driving while balanced on two wheels, over rough ground, adds terrain disturbance exactly where the margin is thinnest.

Training in simulation is what makes this feasible at all, because the alternative — iterating on the physical machine — is the thing the machine's weight forbids. The paper's contribution is as much the demonstration that the simulated dynamics are faithful enough for this class of manoeuvre as it is the controller itself.

### Results

The learned policies autonomously swing the loader up onto two wheels and, while balancing, drive over rough terrain, trace specified trajectories, spin in place, and swing the bucket back and forth before landing safely back on four wheels.

The comparison the authors draw is the one that gives the result meaning: these manoeuvres have previously been accomplished only by a small number of elite human operators, in official world-record performances. That is a different claim from beating a baseline policy. It says the autonomous controller operates in a region of the state space that human skill barely reaches, which is the region where the hydraulic model and the contact model both have to be right.

The work is published in Science Robotics (11 November 2026), which is a signal about the strength of the physical validation rather than about novelty for its own sake.

### Limitations

The abstract reports capability, not statistics. There is no success rate over repeated trials, no characterisation of the conditions under which the policy fails, and no measurement of how much the manoeuvre degrades with payload or ground variation. For a control result at the edge of stability, the failure distribution is arguably the most important number, and it is not in the summary.

The scope is one machine model on one hydraulic architecture. Sim-to-real transfer for heavy equipment depends on identifying parameters — valve deadbands, cylinder friction, chassis compliance — that differ between individual machines and change with temperature and wear. Whether the same recipe transfers to a second loader off the assembly line, without re-identification, is not established here.

And acrobatics is not productivity. The paper demonstrates dynamic capability; it does not demonstrate that the same stack improves cycle time, fuel use or operator safety in a real work cycle, which is the metric a fleet owner buys.

### Industry implications

Construction, mining and material handling face a structural labour problem: experienced operators are scarce, and the machines are dangerous precisely where they are most productive. A learned controller that can hold a loader at the edge of its actuation envelope is the enabling capability for autonomy in those dynamic regimes — not because sites need robots doing wheelies, but because the same model that balances on two wheels is the one that can recover when a loaded bucket shifts unexpectedly on a slope.

The more transferable lesson is about validation economics. The reason this problem was stuck is that you cannot iterate on a five-tonne machine. A simulation pipeline accurate enough to certify a two-wheel manoeuvre is a reusable asset: it lets a manufacturer test a controller change overnight instead of rebuilding a test site, and it turns "is this safe enough to try" into a simulation question.

Expect the near-term commercial form to be operator assistance rather than full autonomy — stability envelopes, load-aware limits, automatic recovery — because those can ship on existing machines with the same dynamic model and without asking a site to accept an unmanned loader.
