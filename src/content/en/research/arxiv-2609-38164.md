*Two things decide whether a robot foundation model earns its place on a factory floor: how well it transfers to the specific arm you own, and how little data it takes to fix the cases where it fails. Rho is built around both.*

### Background

Physical-AI models must generalise broadly and control precisely, and they must do so across the many different robots that actually exist. In practice, though, the models that look strongest are often evaluated on a single embodiment with a single training recipe, and the adaptation story is left vague: teams are told a model is "general" without a clear account of what changes when it meets a new arm or an out-of-distribution situation. Data-light adaptation is the operative problem, because the expensive part of industrial robotics is not training the base model once but repeatedly fixing it for each platform and each long-tail scenario.

### Core Innovation

Rho is a family of open-weights VLA models for bimanual manipulation, developed specifically for data-light task adaptation across three embodiments chosen to represent dual-arm robots in research labs and industry: YAM Box, UR AI Trainer and FR3 Duo. Two design choices carry the contribution. First, the authors systematically ablate the action-expert architecture and the training recipe, and show through controlled simulation and physical-robot experiments that embodiment midtraining - an intermediate training stage on the target platform - measurably improves downstream adaptation. Second, Rho builds online adaptation in by design: an internal latent policy learns from corrective feedback to choose observation-conditioned noise inputs for a frozen flow-matching action expert. Because only this lightweight module is adapted, the cost of learning from a human's corrections stays low.

### Results

- The Rho variants for YAM Box, UR AI Trainer and FR3 Duo match or outperform existing open-weights VLAs, with the strongest overall performance across the tasks, embodiments and baselines evaluated in the report.
- Embodiment midtraining is shown, in controlled simulation and on physical robots, to improve downstream adaptation.
- As few as 15 corrected episodes are enough, when adapting the lightweight latent module, for Rho to handle task situations at the fringe of its offline finetuning distribution.
- The base Rho model and the embodiment-specific checkpoints are released to support both research and practical industrial deployment.

### Limitations

The claims are relative to a defined set of open-weights baselines, tasks and embodiments, so "strongest overall" is scoped to that evaluation rather than to all manipulation. Three dual-arm platforms are covered, which is broader than most single-embodiment releases but still a small slice of the robot population, and the paper does not establish how well the recipe transfers to arms outside those representative designs. The online-adaptation result is framed around situations at the fringe of the training distribution; whether repeated corrections accumulate into durable improvement, or drift or degrade over long operation, is not settled here. Finally, "data-light" still requires a base model and the midtraining stage, so the total investment is lower than from-scratch training but not zero.

### Industry Implications

For anyone integrating manipulation into a production line, the practical value is in the adaptation economics rather than the benchmark crown. A released, open-weights base model with per-embodiment checkpoints removes the need to train a foundation model internally, and a recipe that fixes long-tail failures from roughly fifteen corrected episodes turns integration from a months-long data-collection project into a short, on-site calibration. That is the difference between a model that demos well and one a customer site can actually ship. The scoping caveats matter for planning: teams should expect to run their own embodiment midtraining and to validate that corrections hold up over time rather than assuming the fifteen-episode figure transfers unmodified to their arm and their tasks.
