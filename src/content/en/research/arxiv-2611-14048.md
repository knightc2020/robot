World action models have become one of the dominant paradigms for robotic manipulation over the past year. The intuition is clean: teach a model how the world will evolve, then read the action off that predicted evolution. Video generation models supply a ready-made physical prior, with actions attached either as a conditioning signal or as a jointly modelled output. The approach genuinely buys more temporal understanding and physical common sense than plain imitation learning.

This paper points at a mismatch that is easy to overlook. Whether the model predicts video first and actions second, or models both jointly, existing methods transfer the world model's global physical representation directly to action generation. But a global scene representation is optimised to predict the next frame, so it naturally values lighting, background and overall layout. Grasping a cup depends instead on the relative pose of fingers and rim, the local geometry of contact, the slight deformation as the object is squeezed. A representation tuned for world evolution and one tuned for manipulation are not the same thing.

### Core innovation

LaST0.5 inserts a layer of interaction-centric physical representation between world modelling and action generation. Under supervision from visual interaction cues between robot and environment, it learns a compact interaction latent space that does not reconstruct the whole scene, only what bears on how the robot is currently affecting objects.

Architecturally that representation becomes a latent expert sitting between the world expert and the action expert in a mixture-of-transformers. Existing video-pretrained weights survive intact, while interaction information gets its own capacity and its own compute path. Compared with cramming interaction features into the world representation, the division of labour lets each expert specialise.

The second design is adaptive world-to-action transfer. The model jointly predicts future representations and progress values, then uses progress consistency to adjust action execution and trigger future resampling. Progress acts as a lightweight self-supervisory signal: when executed motion diverges from the predicted world evolution, the current action sequence has drifted and should be re-imagined. That is cheaper than replanning at a fixed rate and more robust to disturbance than open-loop execution.

The third is scale. Pretraining runs on a corpus of more than 14k hours of robot data, a volume that signals an intent to make interaction-centric representation a general paradigm rather than a per-task trick.

### Results

The 99.5% on LIBERO mostly tells you that benchmark is saturating and carries little comparative information on its own. What carries weight is beating the previous state of the art on ten evaluated RLBench tasks, plus real-world demonstrations of long-horizon execution and precise dexterous manipulation. The physical experiments are the harder half of the claim, since real lighting, object poses and contact dynamics all sit outside the comfortable part of the training distribution.

The abstract does not break out per-task numbers, describe the composition of the pretraining corpus, or report inference latency. For a family of methods whose core cost is imagining the future, the missing latency figure is the most conspicuous gap: if every decision requires a multi-step denoising pass, real-time operation becomes the binding constraint, and success rates cannot reveal it.

### Limitations

The source of progress supervision is unstated. If progress labels come from human annotation or an auxiliary estimator, the cost of adaptive transfer is understated; if they come from stage segmentation of demonstrations, whether the signal survives tasks without clean stage structure remains open. Evaluation centres on two simulated benchmarks plus a small set of real-robot experiments, so cross-embodiment and cross-scene generalisation is not yet established. The interaction latent is supervised by visual interaction cues, and such cues degrade under heavy occlusion or with transparent objects; the paper does not discuss how that degradation propagates into action quality. A 14k-hour pretraining corpus also puts reproduction out of reach for academic groups, so comparability will depend on the ablations the authors choose to disclose.

### Industry implications

For teams building general manipulation policies, the architectural lesson is pragmatic: do not assume a world model's global representation carries the local information manipulation needs. Interaction deserves its own representational capacity. That runs against the intuition that a bigger video model automatically yields better control, and it helps explain why some heavily resourced world models look ordinary on fine manipulation.

The progress signal is equally interesting commercially. It turns how far along the task is into a predictable quantity, which is exactly what failure detection, retry triggering and human handover require, and exactly what benchmarks rarely measure. What will decide whether models like this reach a production line is still inference cost and stability in unstructured environments, and this paper leaves both largely unanswered.
