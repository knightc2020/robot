Humanoid loco-manipulation is a coordination problem before it is a learning problem. The robot must walk, keep its balance, and apply controlled force with its hands — often simultaneously, often while something pushes back. Asking a human to teleoperate all of that at once is asking a lot, and the practical consequence is that humanoid demonstration data remains scarce and expensive.

Existing interfaces pick a corner of the trade-off. Whole-body motion capture gives expressive control but requires a suit, a calibrated volume and a skilled performer, and the retargeting from human proportions to robot proportions introduces its own error. Handheld or VR controllers give precise end-effector control but tend to treat the base as a separate vehicle, so the operator's mental model no longer matches a machine that must balance while it reaches.

### Core innovation

FORGE's proposal is to give the operator a command space with granularity, rather than one unified interface. At the coarse end sit velocity navigation commands and planar foot placement: the operator says where the body should go and where to step, and the low-level controller handles balance. In the middle sits wrist-pose tracking, which is where manipulation precision actually lives. At the fine end sit optional force-conditioning inputs, used when the task requires pushing, squeezing or holding a load.

The training side is where the design pays off. Policy training needs no full-body human motion trajectories and no joint-angle references. Deployment consumes only sparse bilateral wrist poses relative to the pelvis, coarse motion commands, and optionally a desired wrist load. That is a dramatically smaller input space than motion capture, and it means the demonstration interface can be a pair of tracked handhelds rather than a suit.

The force channel is the most interesting piece. Rather than instrumenting the wrist, FORGE combines a momentum observer with a learned correction trained under simulator contact-force supervision, and at deployment estimates low-frequency wrist-equivalent interaction forces from proprioception plus reconstructed low-level arm PD torques. For sustained contact — leaning on a box, holding a squeeze — that estimate drives a quasi-static virtual-target loop that adapts the wrist target toward the desired applied force.

Stability is handled by not fighting the locomotion policy. A frozen locomotion prior provides the walking behaviour, and asymmetric residual actions apply manipulation-induced whole-body corrections on top. Freezing the prior is a pragmatic choice: it keeps the legs from being destabilized by a manipulator that suddenly loads the torso.

### Results

The evaluation is deliberately decomposed rather than reduced to one number. The authors report unified-control stability, task-space tracking accuracy, quasi-static wrist-equivalent force estimation, squeezing forces during sustained contact with a rigid box, and finally a downstream diffusion policy executing a squat-and-lift task across payload conditions.

That last item is the most consequential. A teleoperation system is only useful if the data it produces trains policies that transfer, and demonstrating a diffusion policy that lifts a box under varying payload is a direct test of whether the force channel carried real information. The sustained-contact squeezing test is the second: holding a rigid box without a wrist force sensor requires the proprioceptive estimate to be good enough to regulate, not merely to display.

The abstract does not report absolute success rates or force-estimation error in newtons, so the magnitude of each result cannot be judged from the summary alone.

### Limitations

Force estimation is explicitly low-frequency and quasi-static, which is an honest scope statement and also a real constraint: impact, slip onset and fast contact transitions are exactly where a wrist sensor earns its cost, and proprioceptive reconstruction from PD torques will lag there. The evaluation is on one humanoid platform, and the frozen locomotion prior means the system inherits whatever that prior cannot do — uneven terrain, stairs, or recovering from a serious stumble are outside the demonstrated envelope.

There is also an interface question the paper does not fully answer. Sparse bilateral wrist poses relative to the pelvis are a compact command, but they discard the operator's own sense of whole-body configuration, and it is not reported how much practice an operator needs before the mapping feels natural. Finally, all downstream policy evidence comes from a squat-and-lift task; whether these demonstrations suffice for dexterous in-hand work or for tasks requiring precise foot placement under load remains open.

### Industry implications

For anyone building humanoid data pipelines, the message is that the interface, not the policy, is the leverage point. A system that needs only tracked wrists and coarse commands lowers the cost per demonstration and, more importantly, lowers the skill floor for the person collecting it — which is what turns demonstration collection from a research ritual into a shift job.

The force-from-proprioception result matters commercially for a different reason: wrist force/torque sensors are expensive, fragile and the first thing to break on an industrial arm. Recovering usable contact force from joint torque commands and encoder data means a cheaper bill of materials and fewer field failures, and it means existing hardware can be retrofitted with force-aware control through software.

The open strategic question is data reuse. Humanoid teams are converging on the bet that a single generalist policy will absorb demonstrations across many tasks and embodiments. If that bet holds, the value of a cheap, standard demonstration interface compounds, because it determines how much of the world's manipulation experience can be converted into training signal. FORGE is a step toward making that conversion cheap enough to be routine.
