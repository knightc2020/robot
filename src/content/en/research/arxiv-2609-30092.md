The last three years of robot learning have been dominated by one question: can a policy generalize — across scenes, objects, instructions, embodiments? A quieter question has received far less attention: can a policy survive the robot it is attached to? Physical hardware is not stationary. Actuators develop bias, joint encoders drift, and identical robots are never identically calibrated. A policy that is accurate on the machine it was trained on can quietly degrade on the same machine a month later, or on the next unit off the line.

### Background

Vision-language-action (VLA) models are among the most capable generalist manipulation policies available, and they are memoryless by design: each action is produced from the current observation and instruction, with no persistent state about the robot's condition. That is exactly what makes them brittle to test-time environment shifts. Hardware shifts caused by wear or imperfect calibration are especially awkward because they are not visible in the scene — the camera still sees the same object, but the mapping from commanded to realized motion has changed. The conventional remedy is periodic on-site recalibration, which does not scale across a deployed fleet and is precisely the maintenance burden that blocks large-scale real-world rollout.

### Core Innovation

Self-Adaptive VLA is a post-training recipe that lets a policy iteratively adapt to deployment-time hardware shifts by using **its own rollouts as context**. Four ingredients:

- **Collect rollouts under injected shifts.** The policy is run in deliberately shifted conditions, generating data that shows the failure modes the shift induces.
- **Shift-conditioned expert demonstrations.** The base policy's original training data is transformed into expert demonstrations conditioned on the shift, by *pre-compensating* expert actions for the known injected shift. The knowledge of the shift is used to synthesize the supervision the policy needs.
- **A lightweight, plug-in context encoder.** Visual observation, proprioception, and actions in the shifted environment are compressed into a single latent context token. That token modulates the policy through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) — a small addition to a frozen backbone rather than a redesigned model.
- **Ensembled context tokens.** Because the tokens can be ensembled, the policy can iteratively self-correct, mitigating failures step by step rather than committing to one estimate of the shift.

### Results

- Evaluated across **four precision-critical bi-manual and dexterous manipulation tasks**.
- Self-Adaptive VLA **recovers more than 80% of the base policy's performance** under hardware shifts including **actuation bias** and **joint encoder offsets**.
- It also enables **more robust deployment to new workstations** compared with the base policy.

The precision-critical framing matters: bi-manual and dexterous tasks are where small calibration errors turn into grasp failures, so recovering 80%+ of base performance under injected shift is a meaningful bar.

### Limitations

The evidence comes from *injected* shifts with known parameters, evaluated on task success — not from observing real wear over time on a long-running fleet. That distinction matters, because measured actuation drift in the field may not match the injected distributions, and the pre-compensation transform requires knowing the shift, at least approximately. The set of shift types evaluated is small (actuation bias, joint encoder offsets), so generalization to backlash, thermal drift, compliance changes, or actuator degradation is unverified. The abstract does not report how many rollouts are needed per shift or how long the post-training takes, which determines whether this is a maintenance tool or a factory step. No comparison is given against a cheaper baseline such as periodic recalibration or simple proprioceptive input scaling, so the marginal benefit over existing maintenance practice is unclear. Finally, all four tasks are precision-critical bi-manual or dexterous work; whether the same recipe helps long-horizon mobile or locomotion-adjacent policies is untested.

### Industry Implications

For anyone planning to run VLAs on more than a handful of robots, this paper addresses the cost that no amount of policy accuracy eliminates: keeping units calibrated. The economic claim is that hardware drift can be handled at the software level, with a plug-in encoder that learns the current condition from the policy's own rollouts and modulates a frozen model, instead of sending a technician to recalibrate each cell. Two properties make the approach attractive operationally — the base model is not retrained, so the change is reversible and cheap to ship, and the ensembled context tokens turn adaptation into an incremental self-correction loop rather than a single up-front guess. The natural product form is a deployment package where a new workstation is brought online by running a short self-adaptation phase rather than by manual calibration. The caveat is that this becomes a real solution only when it is validated against genuine wear, over time, with the shift estimated rather than injected — which is the study the next paper on this line should run.
