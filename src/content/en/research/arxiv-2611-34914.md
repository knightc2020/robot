The complaint against vision-language-action models is no longer that they cannot manipulate. It is that their competence is welded to the room they were trained in. Move a cup thirty centimetres and the policy still carries it to where the cup used to be; put a box in the corridor and it drives the gripper through it. The obvious repair — fine-tune on the new layout — costs a fresh robot dataset per room and quietly overwrites skills the base model already had.

Cairn attacks the problem at the interface instead of the weights. The VLA stays frozen. What gets added is a small prior that reads a point cloud of the scene and of the held object, the grasp pose at pick-up, and the instruction, and then samples SE(3) trajectories for the object between grasp and release — trajectories that bend around obstacles and rotate the object so it fits through a gap. The prior and the VLA are wired as the two levels of a planner with feedback in both directions: the VLA's intended motion selects and reshapes which trajectory is used, and the chosen trajectory in turn biases the VLA's denoising. Concretely, the predicted end-effector actions are mapped through the grasp to poses of the held object and pulled toward the next waypoint.

That last detail is the load-bearing one. Steering a policy through its action space is normally a fight, because end-effector deltas and object motion are different things once the gripper is closed. By expressing the prior's plan in the frame of the object being carried, the correction becomes a consistent target the denoiser can be nudged toward, action step after action step, without ever touching a parameter.

The scale of the effect on held-out layouts is hard to argue with: 1,941 LIBERO scenes with displaced objects and added obstacles, success going from 24.5% to 95.1% for a frozen pi0.5, which is above the same model fine-tuned on those very layouts. Standard LIBERO is unharmed, which is the point of not fine-tuning. On the third-party LIBERO-PRO benchmark the gain is much smaller — 27.3% to 38.0% — although on the episodes the steering actually engages it moves 54.4% to 81.1%, which says the residue is a routing problem rather than a control problem. The real-robot demonstration is the one that matters commercially: a prior trained entirely in simulation steers a frozen DROID pi0.5 around an obstacle and places an object at the far limit of a Franka's reach, a case where the unsteered policy simply fails.

The boundaries of the claim deserve attention. Everything rests on knowing the held object's geometry and pose, so the method assumes a clean segmentation and a grasp that survives the transit; regrasping, slipping or a compliant or articulated payload is outside what the abstract demonstrates. The trajectory prior is conditioned on the instruction and the scene, and nothing in the summary says how it behaves when the instruction itself is out of distribution, which is likely why LIBERO-PRO stays low. And single-object transport through static clutter is a narrower problem than the long-horizon, multi-object tasks that fill real workcells.

For anyone shipping arms, though, the architecture is the interesting part. It separates a durable foundation policy from a cheap, site-specific steering layer that can be retrained in simulation for each cell, each fixture, each altered aisle — without a data collection campaign and without the skill erosion that comes with fine-tuning. That is a much more tractable maintenance story than retraining the policy every time a factory floor is rearranged.
