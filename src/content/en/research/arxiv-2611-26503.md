Power electronics hardware development is still a human-led craft: design, fabricate, measure, revise, repeat, with an engineer present at every turn. JARVIS does not automate one of those steps. It closes the whole design-build-test-learn loop around circuit boards and runs it unattended.

The interface is deliberately thin. One text instruction goes in; a board that has actually been fabricated, actually been measured, and actually been revised on the basis of that measurement comes out.

### Core innovation

The first design decision is rehearse before you spend material. A robot-mounted 3D scanner maps the workspace, and JARVIS builds an executable digital twin in which it rehearses robot motions, fabrication steps and circuit behaviour. Trial and error moves from the physical world into computation, which saves stock, time and scrapped boards.

The second is modelling machine capability explicitly. The agent maintains machine-specific capabilities and constraints, so what it emits is an executable procedure rather than a process document a human has to interpret. It coordinates unmodified desktop equipment: a robot arm, a PCB printer that deposits conductive silver ink and solder paste, and a pick-and-place machine.

The third is the diagnostic return path. Cameras and sensors verify progress during fabrication; an OPAL-RT real-time simulator and an oscilloscope test the finished circuit. Deviations from the design are diagnosed, a revised design is generated in KiCad, and the next rehearsal begins.

### Results

The demonstration is a 60 Hz notch filter for grid-connected converter sensing. JARVIS completed the first-run experiment in 2 h 39 min without human intervention, achieving 50.6 dB attenuation at 60 Hz.

The more informative numbers come from rehearsal: the digital twin identified five issues before any material was consumed, including a test criterion that would have incorrectly rejected a functioning board. Errors of that kind normally surface only at physical measurement, at the cost of a full prototype round.

The agent also resolved faults autonomously during execution. That detail matters more than it looks — anomalies are routine on real equipment, and unattended operation is only credible if the system can recover from them.

### Limitations

This is a single demonstration: one board, one circuit topology, one successful run. There are no repeated trials and no head-to-head comparison with human engineers on time, yield or cost, so whether 2 h 39 min is fast or slow relative to manual iteration is left to the reader.

Digital-twin fidelity is never quantified. Five issues caught in rehearsal proves the twin is useful; it does not say how many it missed. A non-trivial miss rate would materially weaken the safety margin of the rehearse-first strategy.

The stack is integrated against specific models of robot arm, PCB printer and pick-and-place machine, and swapping hardware means re-modelling capabilities and constraints. Nor is it clear whether multi-board, multi-layer designs with packaged components fall inside the envelope — a passive notch filter is a comparatively simple network.

### Industry implications

For electronics manufacturing and lab automation, the contribution is a reproducible architecture rather than a product: a digital twin for trial and error, robots for execution, instruments for adjudication, and design software for revision, all bound by one task representation. Humans move from executing the loop to supervising it.

For embodied AI research it offers an evaluation angle that manipulation benchmarks miss. The hard part here is not grasp precision but long-horizon state management, reasoning about equipment constraints, and failure diagnosis — capabilities that tabletop pick-and-place suites barely probe.

The economics deserve a caveat. Desktop PCB printing and placement suit small batches and fast iteration — lab prototypes, research instruments, one-off fixtures. For volume production, throughput and consistency are nowhere near replacing an existing line. JARVIS most plausibly lands at the front of R&D as an iteration accelerator, not at the end of manufacturing as a capacity substitute.
