Vision tells a robot where objects are; it does not tell the robot how hard it is pressing or how much load it is carrying. Assembly, connector mating and wiping tasks hinge on exactly that information: the slight bind between a peg and a hole, the click of a plug seating, the normal force holding a cloth against a surface. These are nearly invisible to cameras and decisive for success. A purely visual world action model therefore has a structural blind spot here — it can predict frames, but not the difference between jamming against an edge and sliding cleanly in.

### Core Innovation

CW-WAM takes its cue from hybrid motion-force control: if classical control treats motion and force as two quantities to be regulated together, a world action model should predict them together as well.

The model jointly predicts video, motion and contact wrench. A unified interaction module treats both motion and wrench as modeling targets and emits **time-aligned motion-wrench chunks** as control references, rather than producing actions first and estimating force afterwards. To couple the two signals correctly in time, the authors introduce pair-causal attention over motion-wrench dependencies, plus a fixed-length wrench memory that retains past interaction information — important because instantaneous force readings are noisy and the real signal lives in how force evolves over time.

The predicted wrench sequence is not the final command. It is a force reference handed to a 20 Hz outer-loop admittance controller, forming a slow-fast hierarchy: the world action model is queried at low frequency and owns intent-level motion and force planning, while the admittance controller runs at 20 Hz to hold force feedback and smooth motion between model queries. That division resolves a practical contradiction — large generative models cannot be queried at control rates, but force control must run fast.

### Results

The reported numbers are concrete:

- Square peg insertion with 1 mm clearance per side: **25% → 70%** success;
- Power adapter insertion: **40% → 95%**;
- Force-reference RMSE: **4.8 → 2.7 N** for peg insertion and **2.0 → 1.3 N** for wiping, with smaller force fluctuations during successful execution;
- Lifting-height accuracy also improves across different loads.

The comparison baseline is a WAM without wrench input, so these deltas measure the contribution of the force channel rather than the gap to an unrelated system.

### Limitations

- The baseline is narrow: the main ablation removes the wrench stream from the same architecture, and there is no systematic comparison against dedicated force-control methods such as classical admittance/impedance strategies or tactile servoing.
- A 20 Hz outer loop implies limited force-feedback bandwidth; whether that suffices for stiff contact or fast-impact tasks is not discussed.
- Only insertion and wiping are reported, with no detail on part geometry, clearance distributions, materials or sensor coverage.
- How the admittance controller's stiffness and damping are set — and whether they need per-task tuning — is unstated, yet it directly determines deployment cost.

### Industry Implications

Precision assembly is the most valuable and least automated part of industrial robotics. What makes this result notable is not the absolute 70% but where the gain comes from: not a more expensive tactile sensor or a finer CAD model, but teaching a generative model to predict force and then tracking that prediction with a mature control law. The engineering consequence is that the "intelligence" can sit in a low-frequency prediction layer while high-frequency stability stays with classical control — an incremental upgrade for production arms that already carry force/torque sensors, rather than a ground-up rebuild.

Peg-in-hole at 1 mm clearance is a canonical precision-fit operation. Seventy percent is not enough for unattended operation, but it enters the range where a human supervises and the robot carries most cycles. What will decide whether this reaches a line is whether the remaining 30% of failures are predictable, and whether a new part geometry demands fresh data collection. For teams working on assembly automation, the direction is clear: put force into the policy's prediction targets, not just into the feedback loop's inputs.
