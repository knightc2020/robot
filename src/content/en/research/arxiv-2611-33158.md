Cross-embodiment learning is usually framed as a representation problem: map every robot's actions into one shared space and transfer should follow. The harder issue is that the same task demands different behaviour from different bodies. A two-finger gripper must circle an object looking for a grasp, a long-reach arm can go over an obstacle that a shorter one must go around, and a humanoid may need to crouch before it can reach. A shared action space encodes none of that, so what gets learned is often an averaged solution that is optimal for nobody.

OmniAct starts from letting the policy see its own body. Each robot is represented as a kinematic graph: nodes are joints carrying position and motion axis, edges encode link connectivity, and the whole graph is explicitly aligned with the visual observation of the scene. The policy can therefore reason about which of its joints should move, along which axis, relative to which object. The action head is built on the same structure — a flow-matching policy predicts a relative joint motion for every node rather than emitting a fixed-dimensional vector, which is what lets one model cover robots with different degrees of freedom and different topologies.

Scale supplies the other half of the argument. The authors procedurally design and filter one million physically feasible embodiments, generate manipulation trajectories for all of them in simulation, and train across a diverse set of simulated manipulation tasks. Building bodies first and data second sidesteps the usual bottleneck where real demonstrations exist for only a handful of platforms.

The evaluation is designed to be hard to game. On 2,000 episodes where both embodiment and scene are held out, OmniAct reaches 63.0% success against 57.9% for the strongest baseline, while pi-0.5 manages 17.8% — a gap that says as much about the fragility of generalist VLAs on unseen bodies as about this method. On the task families where GPT-6 Astra was evaluated, OmniAct scores 64.7% against 41.1%. The real-robot result is the more convincing one: on a previously unseen robot it achieves nearly twice the success rate of the next best method, MolmoAct2. The paper also reports a qualitative finding worth noting — the policy learns distinct manipulation strategies that exploit each morphology's strengths and accommodate its limits, rather than replaying one motion pattern on every body.

What to keep in mind: all training data is simulated and real-world validation covers a single unseen robot, so the sample is thin. The kinematic graph depends on accurate joint calibration and URDF information, and wear, compliance and calibration drift in deployed hardware will erode that prior. Force and tactile feedback do not appear in the abstract, leaving contact-rich reliability untested. For industry the implication is concrete: if body descriptions are standardized enough, the deployment cost of serving many robot models with one policy drops sharply — precisely the pain point of vendors shipping several product lines.
