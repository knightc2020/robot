A robot demonstration is worth more than the motion it contains. Every trajectory carries two signals at once: how the action changed the world, and how that change contributed to success. Vision-language-action models capture the semantics of the first, world-action models add future-observation prediction but typically as an auxiliary loss, and the methods that actually improve policies tend to learn dynamics and value in separate models — paying twice for training and blocking any sharing between two genuinely complementary objectives.

DexWAC's bet is that all three belong in one pretraining stage: an actor, an action-conditioned world model, and a bootstrapped critic. The reasoning is that visual prediction forces control-relevant features while value supervision forces task-relevant features, and the representation shaped by both is the one worth building a policy on.

The most interesting engineering choice is that imagination does not require video. Built on an image-editing backbone, the model predicts, for each action chunk, the visual latent and proprioception at the chunk's endpoint together with its reward and continuation flag. A single forward pass therefore produces a complete imagined transition and skips frame-by-frame generation — exactly the cost that has kept video world models out of real-time control loops. After pretraining, the world model and critic are adapted on real rollouts, and the actor is improved either with PPO over imagined rollouts or by critic-guided best-of-N selection.

Empirically DexWAC surpasses large-scale pretrained VLA and world-action baselines in simulation, and reaches 75% average success on two real-world tasks where pi-0.5 gets 45% under the same evaluation. The paper also reports its world model as more accurate and faster than video-based baselines, and ablations confirm that learning dynamics and value together is what improves the policy — a useful check, since joint-training gains are often asserted with weaker controls.

What to hold back on: real-world evaluation covers two tasks, so the 75% figure does not come with a narrow confidence interval. The method inherits the quality of its image-editing backbone and the abstract does not report performance under a swapped backbone. PPO on imagined rollouts remains hostage to world-model error, which compounds over long horizons and can quietly consume the gains. And predicted reward and continuation are a single point of failure for the whole self-improvement loop: biased reward estimates get amplified by the critic straight into the policy.

The industrial implication is a cheaper self-improvement loop. Rather than burning real-robot trials or maintaining a second model just for value learning, one unified model imagines in latent space and only the promising candidates are validated on hardware. For teams that have already accumulated large demonstration corpora, that amounts to a reusable improvement layer on top of data they already own.
