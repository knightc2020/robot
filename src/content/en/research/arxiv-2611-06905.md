Robot foundation models arrive with an awkward contradiction. They are trained to do many things, and they are then adapted to do one thing well. The adaptation step is not optional — zero-shot generalisation, however impressive in a demo, is rarely enough for a production task — but the standard remedy, fine-tuning on deployment data, is the wrong shape for a multi-task model. You take a policy that knows hundreds of behaviours, point it at one task, and optimise. Generality is what you pay for the improvement.

Iterated self-improvement through reinforcement learning is the obvious alternative: let the deployed policy execute, collect its own experience, and update, repeatedly. That story has been demonstrated in single-task settings, where the goal is simply to get better at the one narrow job in front of the robot. What has been missing is the multi-task version. If a generalist policy is going to justify its name in deployment, it should be improving across many diverse tasks at once, not trading breadth for depth one task at a time.

This paper, a large multi-institution collaboration, asks exactly that, and then asks the harder follow-up: what algorithmic recipe makes it work?

### Core innovation

**A structured evaluation suite for generalist improvement, before any method.** The first contribution is methodological. Rather than reporting a single aggregate success rate after online training, the authors build evaluations designed to separate the things that could be going on: improvement on tasks the policy trained on, improvement on nearby tasks, and improvement on tasks it never saw online. Without that separation, a gain on the training distribution and a genuine generalisation gain look identical.

**A systematic sweep of the design axes that actually differ between recipes.** Three choices dominate practical online RL for large policies. Value estimation — how the critic is built and what it is trained on — determines whether the learning signal is usable at all at this scale. Policy extraction — how an updated value function is turned into a new action policy without destroying the pretrained prior — is where naive methods usually fail. Resource allocation — how much online data and optimisation each task gets — is the multi-task question that single-task work never had to answer.

**The findings are conditional, not a single trick.** The paper reports that generalist policies can be improved online across diverse tasks, and — more interesting — that the right recipe increases performance on tasks not considered during online training. It then characterises when that generalisation appears, and identifies two factors as critical: the distribution of tasks used for online training, and the use of value-based RL rather than a purely policy-gradient or behaviour-cloning objective.

### Results

A candid note on evidence: the abstract reports the qualitative findings and the design conclusions but no success-rate numbers, no benchmark names, and no compute figures. For a paper whose contribution is a recipe, that is a real gap for a reader trying to judge effect size. The claims are directional — improvement happens, generalisation happens under specified conditions — and the paper's own framing puts the value in the structured evaluation and the ablation of design choices rather than in a headline number.

What the abstract does establish is the shape of the result: improvement on held-out tasks is possible, and it depends on the composition of the online task set rather than emerging automatically. That second point is the substantive one. A generalist policy does not generalise because it is generalist; it generalises because the online task distribution was chosen to cover the structure that transfers.

### Limitations

The evidence is an empirical study, and empirical studies of online RL on real robots are expensive, which usually means a modest number of tasks and seeds. Without per-task variance in the abstract, it is hard to tell how stable the reported improvements are.

Online RL in deployment has costs the paper's framing does not remove: the robot needs a way to reset, a way to explore safely, and a definition of reward that survives contact with a real environment. Human-in-the-loop supervision is often assumed rather than counted. A recipe that needs a person present for every episode is a very different product from one that runs unattended overnight.

There is also the classic risk of online updates: forgetting. The paper studies improvement on tasks not seen online, which is the encouraging direction, but degradation of previously mastered skills under repeated updates is the failure mode that matters most for a deployed fleet, and the abstract does not address it.

### Industry implications

The strategic point is about where the moat sits. If a generalist policy can be improved in the field across many tasks, then the value of a robot deployment is not just the labour it performs — it is the training signal it generates. That reframes fleet data from a compliance and telemetry cost into the primary asset, and it makes the choice of online task distribution a product decision rather than a research detail.

It also changes what "shipping a policy" means. In the fine-tuning model, you ship a snapshot. In the continual-improvement model, you ship a policy plus a process, and the process has to be safe, monitored, and reversible. That is a heavier engineering commitment, and it is why the recipe question in this paper is worth more than a point of success rate.

For teams currently fine-tuning per task, the practical takeaway is to stop treating generality as the thing you sacrifice at deployment. The evidence here says the sacrifice may be an artefact of the adaptation method, not of the model.
