Six-degree-of-freedom magnetic levitation is attractive on paper: contact-free transport, no wear, micrometre-scale positioning. Its low-level control is notoriously hard. Electromagnetic force is strongly nonlinear in position, the system is open-loop unstable, and the controller has to allocate forces within milliseconds or the mover loses levitation.

Industry's answer has been deep hardware-software co-design. Mover and stator geometries are optimised jointly so that the force law collapses into a compact, hand-derived approximation, which is what makes control computable at kilohertz rates. That trade-off works, but it also confines the operating workspace to the region where the approximation holds and restricts supported mover topologies to the ones the co-design targeted.

Magflow starts from the observation that this is not a physical necessity — it is a historical compute constraint.

### Core innovation

The first contribution is the simulator. Magflow is fully differentiable and computes 6D levitation physics in parallel on a GPU. Differentiability means gradients flow from levitation state all the way back to controller parameters, without finite differences or black-box search; GPU parallelism means thousands of rollouts finish in reasonable time, which is the precondition for any reinforcement-learning or trajectory-optimisation approach.

The second contribution is turning direct physics into a usable training signal. Earlier systems reduced the physics to a hand-crafted approximation and then designed the loop with classical methods; Magflow keeps the full physics and lets a neural network find the control law. The authors describe it as the first neural controller for an industrial-grade 6D levitation system learned purely from simulated physics.

The third is real-time behaviour. The learned policy is not merely validated in offline replay: it satisfies a hard 4 kHz constraint, the actual control rate of the industrial platform, leaving roughly 250 microseconds per inference.

### Results

The decisive evidence is zero-shot transfer. The controller goes from simulation to real hardware without fine-tuning on the device.

Two further results are harder to fake. The learned law exhibits qualitatively new behaviours that hand-designed control does not cover. And it levitates a mover topology for which no hand-crafted solution exists — because the controller no longer depends on an approximation derived for a specific geometry, it can act on mover shapes the co-design process could not support at all.

That last point is the real argument. The topologies excluded by co-design were never physically uncontrollable; the old computational path simply could not afford them.

### Limitations

The abstract describes a single industrial-grade 6D levitation system, with no statistics across platforms, payloads or long runs. One successful zero-shot transfer is a demonstration, but industrial deployment cares about drift after thousands of hours, the effect of thermal rise on electromagnetic parameters, and robustness to component ageing — none of which appear in the abstract.

Training purely in simulation also puts the burden on fidelity. How much eddy-current, hysteresis and saturation modelling is needed before zero-shot transfer holds? The paper reports no force-error comparison between simulation and hardware, and no degradation curve for when transfer fails.

The compute platform behind 4 kHz inference is also unstated. If a high-end GPU is required to sustain that rate, the addressable set of cost- and power-sensitive industrial applications narrows considerably.

### Industry implications

Levitating transport is a real industrial need in semiconductor, display and cleanroom logistics, and its cost structure has long been dominated by having to redo electromagnetic design and control calibration for every new mover. If the controller can be learned in simulation and deployed without tuning, the marginal cost of adding a mover topology falls from a co-design project to a simulation-and-training run.

It also reshapes the division of labour in control engineering. Classical control offers interpretability, provable properties and certifiability; learned control offers the ability to absorb physics that has no compact approximation. The realistic industrial path is probably hybrid: differentiable simulation for design-space exploration and controller pre-training, classical methods for safety envelopes and certification. Magflow supplies the first half of that stack.

Finally, it is a rare positive sim-to-real data point of a specific kind: not manipulation transfer propped up by domain randomisation, but a setting where the physical model can be accurate, the control rate is high and the task is well defined. That combination may be where learned control actually lands in industry first.
