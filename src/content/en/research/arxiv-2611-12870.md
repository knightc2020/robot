World action models predict video and actions jointly, conditioned on language. Most prior work focuses on architecture and training mechanics, implicitly treating the language-video alignment acquired during video pretraining as an established foundation. But introducing action learning can damage that alignment: once the model is optimized for control, the semantic-visual correspondences it learned are reshaped. Principled, reproducible guidance on how to design data and supervision so that action learning preserves language-video ability has been scarce.

SWAM treats the question as experimental science. The authors assemble a pool of roughly 47,000 hours spanning general video, egocentric video, UMI demonstrations and robot demonstrations, then dissect the role of each supervision signal on that pool.

### Core innovation

The first layer is a controlled comparison of supervision forms. The paper examines language-video supervision, language-video-action supervision, and their joint use, turning "does action learning hurt alignment" from speculation into a measurable quantity.

The second is annotation quality. The authors study how the quality of language annotations affects pretrained language-video capability. That has direct consequences for pipelines relying on automatic or semi-automatic labelling: if annotation noise mainly damages semantic alignment rather than control precision, the data budget should be allocated differently.

The third is the source of action supervision. The paper analyses how action supervision from UMI and robot demonstrations extends language-video capability into language-video-action modelling. UMI occupies a useful middle ground between human demonstration and real robot data, and its value is assessed explicitly as part of the recipe.

On top of this, the authors compare joint-training recipes and architectures. One concrete finding: under a fixed training budget, mixing egocentric video with UMI demonstrations improves control generalization; architecture determines how efficiently those experiences are used, and further pretraining scale under the selected configuration brings additional gains.

### Results

The final recipe is post-trained only on RoboTwin Clean demonstrations. It reaches 88.10% success under Easy (Clean-to-Clean) and 61.56% under Hard (Clean-to-Random), indicating that the generalization comes from the recipe rather than from piling on randomized data.

Real-world evaluation covers three tasks and reaches 40.00% mean success against 23.33% for the strongest baseline — nearly double — which the authors attribute to better generalization under unseen conditions. The curated data, training code and model weights are released, which is uncommon in this area and lowers the barrier to reproduction and secondary development.

### Limitations

The abstract reports only aggregate numbers: no per-task results, variance or multi-seed statistics. Three real-world tasks are a small sample, and a 40% absolute success rate shows physical deployment still has substantial headroom. Building and training on a 47,000-hour pool is expensive, and it is unclear how much of the gap the released data closes for smaller organisations. The paper discusses annotation quality but the abstract gives no quantitative alignment metric, so the mechanism by which action learning degrades alignment remains only partially characterised.

### Industry implications

The immediate value is an actionable recipe: egocentric human video plus UMI demonstrations improves control generalization on a limited budget, and Clean-only post-training transfers to randomized conditions, so data collection can be more focused. For industry teams this lowers the "build a large in-house robot dataset" barrier and shifts competition toward data mixing, annotation quality and training strategy — variables that are easier to control. Open weights also let smaller teams adapt a strong base to vertical tasks without pretraining from scratch. The open question is whether the recipe holds on more real tasks and longer-horizon manipulation.
