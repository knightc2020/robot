Vision-language-action evaluation has settled on a narrow contact assumption: the gripper touches the object and the object follows. Gravity, friction, inertia and mass distribution — the quantities that actually decide whether a manipulation succeeds — barely appear. So it has been hard to say whether these policies understand physics or interpolate a high-dimensional training distribution.

PhyDEX picks a test with no escape hatch: throwing. The object has to land at a destination outside the arm's kinematic workspace, which means the robot must first infer how heavy the thing is, where its center of mass sits, and how slippery it is, then choose release speed, angle and timing. Get any one of those wrong and the landing point misses.

### Core innovation

The scale is adequate: 35K trajectories, 143 hours, nine tasks, 100 scenes. Objects come in three flavours — uniform, irregular, and dynamic-mass bodies whose distribution shifts during flight — and destinations in three — static, multiple, moving. Crossing those factors separates "same object, different physics" from "same physics, different target," which makes failures attributable to perception, inference or control rather than lumped together.

All data comes from a scalable automatic pipeline combining grasp generation, motion planning and closed-loop throw optimization under randomized physical properties. Automation means the set can be grown on demand, and it means the physical parameters are known exactly — the precondition that makes the diagnostic experiment possible.

That experiment is the real design contribution: feed the model the true mass, center of mass and friction. If success jumps, the bottleneck is physical inference rather than action execution. It does jump, roughly doubling.

### Results

GR00T N1.7, DynamicVLA and ForceVLA all finish below 3% success. That is not "somewhat worse"; it is close to total failure.

The authors split the failure into two bottlenecks. First, pretrained robustness: most failures happen before the throw, with grasping and alignment collapsing upstream. Second, physical blindness proper — supplying ground-truth physical properties roughly doubles success, which says the policy knows how to throw but not what it is holding.

Importantly, the same failure patterns appear on a humanoid robot and in real-world experiments, which rules out a simulator artefact as the explanation for the sub-3% numbers.

### Limitations

The 3% figure is striking, but it measures performance on PhyDEX, not VLA competence in general. Throwing is a deliberately extreme task and a low success rate is partly the point.

The entire benchmark is synthesized, and the sim-to-real physics gap is never quantified. The real-world work reports "the same failure patterns" without real-robot success numbers, so absolute difficulty outside simulation is unknown.

Nor does the abstract state what success becomes after the ground-truth parameters are supplied. If "roughly doubles" means 2% to 4%, the finding stands but its industrial weight shrinks considerably. That missing absolute number is the weakest link in an otherwise clean argument.

### Industry implications

For teams building manipulation policies, the practical value is a cheap self-check. If a policy scores near zero on throwing, the fix is probably not another attention layer; first establish whether it can infer physical parameters at all. Predicting mass, center of mass and friction as explicit intermediates may buy more than additional depth.

For evaluators, the paper names a systematic blind spot. A benchmark built only on static pick-and-place will confuse "looks like it is doing the right thing" with "understands the physics," and only tasks that require extrapolating physical quantities separate the two.

For the humanoid sector the message is sobering but useful: do not plan on end-to-end VLAs autonomously handling high-dynamic throwing or catching work in the near term. The safer engineering route is a separate physical-parameter estimator feeding the policy — at least until data scale crosses some threshold nobody has yet located.
