The promise of code as policies is straightforward: a coding agent writes a program that drives the robot, the program runs, and the reasoning invested in writing it is amortized over every subsequent execution. Compared with a vision-language-action policy that must be queried at every step, the economics are attractive — no inference cost per action, no latency per decision, and a skill that a human can read, diff and audit.

The obstacle has never been the writing. It is that the agent has no reliable way to know whether the program it just wrote will work on the physical robot, and every experiment costs minutes of hardware time plus a human supervisor. Iterate a few dozen times and the amortization argument collapses.

### Core innovation

ACE-CaP-0 is a recipe for spending that experimental budget well. Its two mechanisms are Demonstrations on Request and Simulation on Demand, and their asymmetry is the interesting part.

Demonstrations on request means the agent can ask a human operator to physically guide the robot through a motion it cannot confidently synthesize — a grasp approach, a bimanual handoff, a compliant insertion. This is expensive in human time but extremely high in information: it resolves precisely the ambiguity that caused the failure, in the real physics where it occurred. Simulation on demand means a simulator is available, but it is used selectively, during refinement of a policy that is already meant for the real world, rather than as the primary training ground.

That ordering runs against the current instinct to simulate first and transfer later. The authors' evidence supports it: for this class of task, a requested demonstration buys more reliable progress per unit of effort than a simulated trial, because the residual error being corrected is usually contact and perception detail that the simulator does not reproduce faithfully.

The second mechanism is architectural. The output is frozen executable code, not a policy that calls a model. Perception and control run at execution time, but the reasoning is compiled away. That is what makes the reported execution times meaningful: there is no per-decision inference to be slow or to vary.

### Results

Across eight real-world manipulation tasks spanning alignment, multi-object handling and bimanual coordination, the frozen programs achieve 92.1% success, 221 of 240 trials, with mean execution times between 0.97 and 2.27 minutes and no human or coding agent in the loop.

Those are deployment-shaped numbers. A 92% success rate over 240 trials is enough to characterize a task's reliability rather than merely demonstrate it, and the trial count per task — roughly thirty — is enough that a single lucky run cannot carry the average. The runtime range also tells us something the abstract does not state directly: the tasks differ by more than a factor of two in duration, which suggests the set includes genuinely long sequences rather than eight variations of pick-and-place.

### Limitations

The failure mode matters more than the headline. Eight tasks is a narrow slice of manipulation, and the tasks were presumably chosen because the approach can solve them; nothing here establishes performance on tasks where the perception stack, rather than the policy structure, is the bottleneck. Frozen programs are also brittle by construction: a program written for one table height, one lighting condition and one object instance will not adapt, and the paper does not report how much re-writing a scene change forces. Demonstration-on-request assumes a human is available during development, which is fine for a lab and less fine for a deployment team iterating on a customer site.

There is also a selection effect worth naming. The agent decides when it needs a demonstration and when it needs simulation, and that decision is itself unvalidated; a weaker model might not know what it does not know, and the recipe's performance may depend on the strength of the underlying coding model more than the abstract admits. Finally, 92.1% is not 100%, and for a program that runs unattended the interesting quantity is the distribution of the remaining 7.9% — whether those are recoverable near-misses or silent corruptions of the workspace.

### Industry implications

The most useful claim here is economic rather than algorithmic. If a task can be brought to 92% success with a bounded number of real trials and then executed as frozen code in about a minute, the unit economics of automation change for small-batch work: fixture-heavy classical automation cannot justify its engineering cost below a certain volume, and per-step VLA inference is expensive at any volume. Frozen code policies sit in between, and this paper is an argument that the middle is a real place to be.

The Demonstrations-on-Request pattern also suggests a concrete product shape: a robot that knows when to ask. Not a robot that waits for a human at every step, and not one that improvises until it breaks something, but one that recognizes a specific unresolved ambiguity and requests thirty seconds of guidance. That is a much more tractable interaction design than full teleoperation, and it maps onto how factories already use setup technicians.

What the paper cannot yet answer is where the ceiling is. Code policies inherit the perception stack they call, so their reliability is bounded by whatever object detector and pose estimator sit underneath. As those improve, the ceiling moves, and the case for compiling reasoning into code gets stronger each time.
