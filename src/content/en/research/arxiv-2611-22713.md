Whole-body loco-manipulation has settled into a scale-for-capability trade. The strongest policies carry billions of parameters and still need task-specific fine-tuning for every new behaviour. That works, but it outsources a basic question to data volume: the model has to rediscover, from thousands of trajectories, which joints are connected, how far each leg can reach, and how an arm movement shifts the centre of mass.

Humans take a different route. With sharply limited compute, people still organise perception and action through a body schema — seeing a target already implies which hand to use, from what approach angle, and what the rest of the body must do to support it. Body Schema Policy (BSP) tries to build that prior into the vision-action interface rather than hoping it emerges.

### Core innovation

The first layer is a latent body schema. Joints and links become tokens arranged on the robot's kinematic tree, and learned screw motions carry features between adjacent links. Feature aggregation therefore follows the mechanical structure instead of mixing everything in a body-agnostic global attention pool. For a serial chain, that amounts to hard-coding the "what connects to what" prior into the network topology.

The second layer is body-queried vision. Each body part uses its own query to pull the image evidence relevant to it, and that evidence conditions the denoising of its own actions. Compared with the usual pattern of pooling one global visual token and feeding it to the whole policy, action generation is decomposed into local decisions, each looking only at the pixels that matter for it.

The third layer is throughput. BSP emits action chunks faster than every policy it is compared against, according to the abstract. That matters as much as parameter efficiency: in deployed whole-body control, inference latency usually becomes the binding constraint well before offline success rate does.

### Results

On nine simulated Unitree G1 whole-body tasks, BSP matches the Ψ0 humanoid foundation model while using 75 times fewer parameters, and nearly doubles Diffusion Policy's success rate.

The more interesting result is an interpretability by-product. Without any spatial supervision, the model develops compact, body-part-specific visual readouts: manipulation commands depend strongly on the task object, while locomotion commands respond more broadly to the scene. That is the division of labour one would want — hands are governed by what they are about to touch, legs by terrain and clearance — and here it falls out of the interface rather than being forced by an auxiliary loss.

The same conclusions are reported on a real Unitree G1 and on a Fetch mobile manipulator, which suggests the structure is not tied to a single simulator or embodiment.

### Limitations

Embodiment coverage is still thin: nine G1 tasks in simulation, and a G1 plus a Fetch on hardware. The abstract gives no count of real-world tasks, no per-task success rates and no failure analysis, so "it also holds on real robots" is currently a directional claim rather than a performance table.

The method assumes a known, well-calibrated kinematic tree. The schema's skeleton comes from the manufacturer's model; if link parameters are unknown, joints have backlash, or the tool is swapped, the prior must be rebuilt, and the paper does not examine that degradation.

BSP also matches Ψ0 rather than beating it, and the baselines cover only Ψ0 and Diffusion Policy. Without comparisons against other parameter-efficient designs, the generality of the 75x figure remains untested.

### Industry implications

The first implication is a compute budget. Whole-body policies have been treated as workloads that demand large accelerators; BSP suggests a meaningful share of those parameters were paying for a missing structural prior. Writing the kinematic tree into the interface can shrink the model to something an edge board can serve, which matters for cost-sensitive service robots and for humanoids moving into volume production.

The second is the cost of changing bodies. If structure lives in the interface instead of being learned, porting a policy to a new platform means editing the schema's skeleton, not retraining a billion-parameter model. That re-couples mechanical design, kinematic calibration and policy training — an organisational change as much as a technical one.

The third is data. A structural prior substitutes for some demonstration volume, so a small team no longer needs a large teleoperation corpus before starting whole-body work. The moat shifts from whoever has the most data to whoever designs body, interface and control loop as one system.
