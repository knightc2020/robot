Multimodal models can now drive a robot from images in a zero-shot setting, and the demonstration is convincing enough that the remaining gap is easy to name: images do not show force. Every task that involves pressing, inserting, wiping, or handing something over depends on a quantity the model cannot see, and a policy that only sees pixels will push until something breaks or until the object slips.

The naive fix — expose force as another input — runs into two problems that this paper takes seriously. First, contact evolves on a millisecond timescale, while an API-hosted model takes hundreds of milliseconds to return a decision; a controller that waits for the model before reacting to a force spike is already too late. Second, a frozen model has no idea what to do with a raw force trace. Hand it a stream of Newton values and it has no calibration, no units, and no notion of what a plausible value looks like for this gripper on this object.

### Core innovation

ForceHarness is a harness rather than a model, and its design follows directly from those two constraints.

To close the loop faster than the model can decide, the authors introduce self-terminating force primitives. Each primitive is a short, parameterised force behaviour — press until a threshold, hold a load, retract at a contact event — that regulates itself and returns control to the model when the contact event it was waiting for occurs. Force is therefore closed at the primitive level at control rate, while the model only makes the discrete decisions: which primitive, with which parameters, and what to do with the result.

To make force legible to a frozen model, a physics translation layer converts each force trace into physical quantities with associated uncertainty, and returns that as the tool result. The model never sees raw sensor values; it sees something like an estimated contact force and a confidence, expressed in terms it can reason about. Calibration is handled separately: hand force is recovered from a wrist sensor when one exists, or from joint torques alone when it does not.

The authors also build HiddenPhys, a seven-task MuJoCo benchmark in which physical variables — mass, friction, compliance — are hidden from the policy. It exists because the standard force benchmarks do not test the thing that matters here, which is whether the system can infer hidden physical state from contact rather than memorise a trajectory.

### Results

The headline number is on real hardware. On a real arm with no wrist force sensor, ForceHarness raises the zero-shot success rate of GPT-6 Astra from 3.3% to 90.0%. Two features of that result matter more than the absolute value: force was estimated from joint torques, so no extra hardware was added, and nothing was fine-tuned, so the model's weights are unchanged from the API.

In simulation, the method adds 17.5 percentage points on the public ForceBench suite and 34.3 points on HiddenPhys. On both benchmarks it surpasses force-aware policies that were trained on demonstrations, and on HiddenPhys it matches hand-written expert controllers. Beating a demonstration-trained specialist with a frozen general model plus a harness is the strongest claim in the paper, because it says the bottleneck was interface design rather than policy capacity.

The ablation is the most transferable finding: smaller models make significantly better decisions when given physical quantities than when given raw force values. That suggests the translation layer is not a convenience for weak models but a genuine precondition for using force at all.

### Limitations

The method's competence lives in the primitives. A self-terminating press-and-hold is a good abstraction for insertion and wiping; it is not obvious how it extends to tasks where the useful force profile is itself the thing to be discovered, such as manipulating a deformable object whose compliance changes as it deforms.

Calibration from joint torques alone is an approximation, and the paper's real-robot evidence is one task on one arm. Whether torque-based force estimation survives a geared industrial arm with substantial friction and backlash is an open question, and the failure mode there would be quiet: the model would receive confidently wrong physics.

HiddenPhys is simulation, with seven tasks. It is a well-motivated benchmark but a small one, and sim-to-real for contact is precisely where force models are weakest.

### Industry implications

The immediate implication is that the "frozen API model" architecture is more capable than it looked. Teams that cannot fine-tune a frontier model — because of cost, access, or the fact that the model is someone else's product — can still get contact-rich behaviour by investing in the harness: primitives, calibration, and a translation layer. That is engineering work a robotics team already knows how to do, and it does not require a training cluster.

The second implication concerns where value accumulates. If the harness is what converts a general model into a competent manipulator, then the durable asset is the primitive library and the calibration procedure, not the policy weights. That is a more familiar kind of moat for the robotics industry, and it favours companies with real hardware experience over companies with model access alone.
