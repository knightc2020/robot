Most work a humanoid is meant to do is work humans already do. What is missing is not a task list but supervision that says how the whole body moves while the task is performed. Existing egocentric video datasets cover plenty of daily activity yet rarely ship synchronized whole-body motion, which leaves loco-manipulation learning dependent on small sets of teleoperated robot episodes.

Awaken-Human addresses that layer directly: roughly 50,000 hours of egocentric stereo video paired with simultaneously captured whole-body human motion, spanning more than 100 skills. The video supplies what the world looks like; the motion capture supplies what the body does; both sit on the same time axis.

### Core innovation

The architectural bet in Awaken-HI is a shared action expert. The model jointly predicts the original human motion and the retargeted robot action, with both outputs flowing through one action expert instead of two heads bolted together. Human trajectories are therefore not an auxiliary pretext task that gets discarded — they land on the exact pathway the robot executes at deployment.

The authors' claim is specific: loco-manipulation skills transfer from people to humanoids more effectively when human trajectories directly supervise the shared expert. That contrasts with the two-stage recipe of learning human motion first and distilling it into a robot policy later, where an extra representation hop hides retargeting error inside a second network.

The second contribution is empirical rather than architectural. Increasing human pretraining data reduces held-out robot action prediction error monotonically and raises mean success on downstream real-robot tasks. For a data-engineering team that converts "how many more hours should we collect" from a judgement call into an extrapolatable curve.

### Results

After full-corpus pretraining and adaptation to the T800 platform, Awaken-HI averages 93.75% success across eight individual tasks and outperforms GR00T N1.7 and pi-0.5 in both individual and long-horizon evaluations. The long-horizon comparison carries more weight: error compounds across stages there, so staying ahead implies the policy is not merely reproducing short action fragments it has seen.

Generalization is reported in two forms — objects unseen during robot post-training, and new pairings of familiar objects with familiar destinations. The second is the more interesting test, since it probes recomposition at the task level rather than robustness to visual appearance.

### Limitations

The abstract reports only the eight-task mean. There is no per-task breakdown or failure taxonomy, and no statement of baseline parameter counts or whether training data was matched, so the margin over GR00T N1.7 and pi-0.5 cannot be fully audited.

How much real-robot data the T800 adaptation consumed, and whether adaptation meant full fine-tuning or partial unfreezing, is not specified — precisely the detail that decides whether teams without large compute can reuse the recipe. Inference latency, model size and training cost are also absent, and the collection and annotation cost behind 50,000 hours is passed over.

A structural bias in the corpus deserves scrutiny too: first-person human video skews toward indoor daily activity, with unclear coverage of heavy hauling, precision assembly, or work in heat and weather. Transfer to those settings remains untested.

### Industry implications

What is worth copying here is the data recipe, not the network. The scarce artifact for humanoid teams is a corpus where video, whole-body motion and skill labels are aligned simultaneously — and humans are the largest available source of exactly that. Putting retargeting inside the training objective rather than in a preprocessing pipeline is the cheaper path.

The scaling law also changes procurement logic. If error falls smoothly with human hours, data collection stops being a project expense and becomes a depreciable asset, worth extending skill by skill over years instead of re-collecting demonstrations for every new task.

One caveat should be held firmly. Retargeting is bounded by morphological mismatch: human hands, arm lengths, joint limits and torque budgets do not match a humanoid's gripper and actuators. The 93.75% figure belongs to the T800, and whether the curve keeps the same slope on a different body is not answered here.
