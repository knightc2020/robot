*A policy that picks up objects flawlessly in a laboratory can fail completely on the first shelf it meets in a real home. The question this paper answers is how to personalize it without sending a robot to every user's building to collect demonstrations.*

### Background

Generalist vision-language-action (VLA) policies have become the default route to flexible manipulation: one multimodal model that maps language, images and robot state to actions. But generality learned on broad data does not transfer for free into a specific building. The authors measure the gap directly, and it is stark: a strong generalist such as pi-0.5 achieves 0% pickup success in their captured deployment scenes without adaptation. Closing that gap by collecting real demonstrations in each user's environment, on each user's objects, is prohibitively expensive; a fleet of homes and offices cannot be hand-taught one shelf at a time. The paper's premise is that the expensive part, the human in the loop, can be reduced to a phone scan and a few photographs.

### Core Innovation

Splat2Act is an automated real-to-sim-to-real pipeline. From a handheld phone scan it builds a photorealistic digital twin of the whole building using 3D Gaussian Splatting plus collision geometry. Unlike earlier real-to-sim work that reconstructs a single tabletop, it operates at building scale: it automatically discovers tabletop and non-tabletop receptacles, constructs feasible pickup tasks on them, and generates successful demonstrations with a privileged simulation oracle. The only human contribution is capturing the scene and the objects; scale does the rest. Applied to 101 scanned campus environments with a median navigable area of 104.87 m^2, the pipeline yields 852 receptacles and more than 68,000 pickup demonstrations, a corpus no manual collection effort would match. Fine-tuning of pi-0.5 then proceeds in two stages. Stage 1 adapts to hundreds of public object assets, drawn either from the deployment scene alone or from all 101 scenes. Stage 2 personalizes to the user's own objects on the deployment scene's receptacles.

### Results

- The strongest personalized policy reaches 62.5% aggregate pickup success in the target scene, against the 0% the unadapted generalist manages there.
- Deployed on a real Stretch 3, the same policy achieves 63.9% success on a 12-task subset, closely tracking its 66.7% simulated success on those tasks.
- The pipeline is validated across 101 scanned environments (median 104.87 m^2 navigable area, about 7 receptacles each), producing 852 receptacles and over 68,000 demonstrations.
- Everything above is learned from simulation-oracle data; no real-robot demonstrations in the target scene are required.

### Limitations

The success rate is real but partial: roughly six in ten pickups succeed in the target scene, so the policy is a large step above zero rather than a solved system. The approach still depends on the user performing a scan and photographing objects, and on the quality of the Gaussian-splat digital twin and its collision geometry; a scene that reconstructs poorly may not yield usable tasks or demonstrations. Stage-2 personalization is evaluated per deployment scene, and the paper does not establish how well a personalized policy holds up as objects are added, moved, or replaced after training. The two-stage recipe also inherits whatever biases live in the simulation oracle and the public object assets, since those set the ceiling for the demonstrations.

### Industry Implications

The practical message is about the economics of personalization. If a phone scan plus object photos is enough to lift a generalist from 0% to roughly 60% on a user's own objects, then personalization stops being a per-customer engineering project and becomes a data pipeline that can run at fleet scale, which is exactly what consumer and office robotics deployments need. It also reframes what a deployment team ships: not a single generalist, but a generalist plus an automated scene-capture-and-finetune loop. The residual error rate is the caveat to plan around, and it argues for keeping a fallback such as teleoperation or a human confirm step in early fleets. The building-scale digital twin is itself a reusable asset: capture once, then generate tasks and demonstrations for many future policies and objects.
