Mobile-robot planning lives with a compromise that everyone knows and few enjoy. Approximate the vehicle as a disk and planning is fast but conservative, sometimes comically so. Check the exact footprint and paths are safe, but every candidate motion, pose and footprint edge carries a cost that grows with geometric complexity. For vehicles with non-convex bodies — forklifts, scissor lifts, robots with trailers or manipulator arms — the safe option is often too slow for a useful planning cycle.

TPS-A* attacks the cost structure directly rather than trading safety for speed. It is a lattice planner whose edges are kinematically feasible trajectories produced by parameterized trajectory generators, with collision checking performed in trajectory parameter space.

### Core innovation

The key object is a grid precomputed per footprint. In a single pass over the local obstacle set, that grid returns a certified free distance for every candidate trajectory. Because the computation is organized by footprint and trajectory family rather than by individual pose, the per-node collision cost becomes independent of both footprint complexity and the number of trajectories considered.

The word "certified" is load-bearing. The authors prove the grid never overestimates the free distance of the continuously swept, possibly non-convex footprint. That is the difference between a fast heuristic and a planner with guarantees: the method retains collision-free paths and bounded suboptimality relative to its lattice abstraction, rather than merely being empirically safe in the tested worlds.

Goal reaching is handled exactly. Reeds-Shepp shots are certified as trajectories of the same generator family, so goals with or without a heading requirement are reached precisely, without a separate, unverified connection step. An exact independent test finds no collision in the planner's paths — a useful check, because collision checkers are exactly where quiet bugs hide.

### Results

The evaluation is designed to be hard to game: 300 cluttered worlds, the true footprint for every planner, and the same goal pose across comparisons. TPS-A* solves every query with a collision-free goal pose in a median of 3 ms on an AMD Ryzen 7 2700X.

The comparison that matters is not raw speed but speed at equal safety. It is on par with hybrid A* — while 45% of hybrid A*'s paths overlap obstacles. Against a state-lattice planner, the only other collision-free method in the comparison, it is nearly sixty times faster; with position-only goals it is eighty-nine times faster than hybrid A*.

The qualitative cases are well chosen. It matches hybrid A* in automated parking and narrow doorways, docks a non-convex fork footprint that no convex model admits, and drove a real robot across a university campus among people. The last item is not a benchmark, but it is the kind of demonstration that separates a planner from a paper.

### Limitations

Bounded suboptimality is still suboptimality. The lattice abstraction constrains the paths the planner can represent, and the bound depends on the lattice and generator set; tighter spaces or unusual kinematics may need finer lattices and lose some of the speed advantage.

The method assumes the obstacle map is available and reasonably current. Campus and warehouse environments with people are dynamic in ways a static local obstacle set does not capture, and the paper's real-world demonstration is a route traverse rather than a study of dynamic-obstacle negotiation.

Precomputation is per footprint, which is fine for a fixed vehicle but less convenient for a reconfigurable one — a forklift with moving tines, or a mobile manipulator whose arm changes the swept shape. The abstract does not report how the grid scales with footprint resolution or how often it must be rebuilt.

Finally, the comparison set is 2D ground vehicles. The idea of certified distance in trajectory parameter space might extend to 3D, but nothing here demonstrates it.

### Industry implications

For AMR and AGV fleets, the practical consequence is that exact-footprint planning stops being a luxury. A 3 ms median on a desktop CPU leaves room for replanning inside a normal control loop, which means vehicles no longer have to choose between conservative disk models in tight aisles and expensive exact checks that force slow cycles.

Non-convex footprints are the quieter win. Forklifts, tow tractors and robots carrying loads are exactly the machines that a disk model serves badly, and the fork-footprint docking demonstration targets that gap. Warehouses full of such vehicles have long accepted extra clearance as the price of tractability; certified checking removes that tax.

The broader lesson is architectural: when the expensive part of a planner can be precomputed against geometry rather than recomputed per query, the planner's role changes. It becomes cheap enough to run frequently, closer to a reactive layer, and the safety argument rests on a proof rather than on a margin chosen by hand.
