Robot policies have been splitting into two camps. Video-based world-action models generate future frames and actions together, which buys generalization and data efficiency at the price of a video generator. Latent-space policies avoid pixels but usually align intermediate features to some frozen target encoder, which decouples future prediction from the representation the VLM actually reasons with. Z-sight takes the obvious-in-hindsight position: if the VLM perceives the present in its own visual token space, let it imagine the future there too.

The unification is not rhetorical. The prediction head neither reconstructs pixels nor regresses toward an external encoder's features; it predicts the spatial tokens the VLM would see next. Perception and foresight share one coordinate system, and a semantic translation step disappears from the middle of the stack.

### Core innovation

The architecture is a mixture-of-transformers: a VLM backbone for multimodal understanding, a foresight expert that rolls out future spatial tokens, and a continuous action expert that emits action chunks. At inference the future tokens and the actions are denoised jointly, so action generation reads the intermediate state of "what the scene becomes if I do this" rather than consuming a finished prediction.

The second choice is refusing pixel reconstruction. Most of the cost in video world models sits in decoding back to pixels; keeping supervision at the token level removes the generator's parameters and latency, and avoids a reconstruction objective that rewards looking right over being useful for control.

Training mixes human video with robot trajectories: human video supplies priors on how scenes evolve, robot trajectories bind actions to their visual consequences. That mixture is plausibly what allows the method to transfer to a high-DoF humanoid without collapsing.

### Results

On SIMPLE it reaches 81.9% success, 9.6 points above the strongest baseline. The more telling number is the six Unitree G1 tasks, where it averages 73.3%, a 20.0-point gain. Humanoids have large action spaces, hard balance constraints and noisy egocentric observations, so a 20-point jump is evidence that the representation survives the move to a high-DoF body rather than overfitting to tabletop arms.

Efficiency matters as much as accuracy: 110 ms per action chunk keeps joint denoising inside the range where chunked control is practical. The authors also report remaining competitive on tabletop benchmarks, so architectural unification was not bought by regressing on mature settings.

### Limitations

The abstract gives no per-task success breakdown or failure taxonomy for the six G1 tasks, and does not state baseline parameter counts or whether training data was matched, so the 20-point claim cannot be fully audited.

At 110 ms per chunk the method is fine for pick-and-place but still slow for highly dynamic work such as catching, fast evasion or close human-robot collaboration; that boundary is not discussed. Nor is the length of the foresight horizon, or whether future-token error accumulates over long-horizon tasks.

### Industry implications

For humanoid teams the headline is budgetary: a world model does not have to mean a separate video generator. Compressing prediction into the VLM's own token space keeps parameter count, memory and latency manageable, and removes the need to maintain three separate representations for perception, prediction and control.

The longer-term implication is architectural. The visual token space of a VLM is itself a usable prediction space, which shifts competition from whether a policy has a world model to what representation that model lives in, how far it looks ahead, and how its error is corrected.

One caveat deserves emphasis: a token-space future is semantic, not metric. Whether this is precise enough for millimetre-level assembly and insertion is not answered by the experiments here.
