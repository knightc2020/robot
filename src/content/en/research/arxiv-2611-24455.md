Vision-language-action policies rest on an assumption that is rarely stated and constantly violated: the third-person camera must stay exactly where it was during data collection. A bumped tripod, a remounted camera, or a robot moved to the next cell leaves the task and the machine unchanged while success falls off a cliff. Homeward is about that silent assumption.

What makes the paper interesting is its diagnosis. Simulation reveals a paradox: with oracle geometry, re-rendering a perturbed view back into the training camera recovers most of the lost performance, but substituting an off-the-shelf feed-forward 3D foundation model for that re-rendering can leave the policy worse than doing nothing. The degradation concentrates on the thin, articulated arm, whose edges a feed-forward depth predictor tears and scatters.

### Core innovation

The insight is a division of labour: estimate what is unknown, render what is known.

Camera parameters and static scene depth are unknown, and Homeward recovers them from the live RGB frame plus a single reference image from the training view. The arm's 3D state, by contrast, is already known — forward kinematics maps joint angles to every link pose — so the arm is rendered directly from the kinematic model instead of being guessed by a depth network that will smear it.

The consequence is an adapter that needs no scene-specific fine-tuning, no deployment-time calibration, and no modification to the frozen policy; it sits at the observation inlet. With per-step pose estimation it also handles 6-DoF random placements, focal-length changes, and slow camera drift within an episode.

### Results

Scale is the most persuasive part. The evaluation spans roughly 300,000 closed-loop episodes, four frozen VLA policies and 1,599 camera variants of LIBERO-Plus. Success rises from 62.5% to 77.6% for OpenVLA-OFT, from 76.5% to 95.1% for pi0.5, from 68.0% to 91.2% for pi0, and from 3.6% to 26.4% for OpenVLA, which was effectively broken before.

On LIBERO-Plus, three of the four policies match or beat a calibrated, scene-trained view-synthesis baseline. That matters: calibration-free estimation plus kinematic rendering is not buying robustness by sacrificing accuracy.

### Limitations

The method assumes a static scene. In a plant with moving objects, walking people or passing AGVs, depth estimation and canonicalization both degrade, and the evaluation is confined to tabletop manipulation.

It needs one training-view reference image, so deployments must retain a snapshot of the original scene; if the cell has been rearranged, the reference itself is stale. The extra latency of per-step pose estimation is not reported and needs measuring on a real control loop.

The gains are large but OpenVLA still lands at 26.4%, a reminder that canonicalization fixes only the viewpoint, not the policy. Viewpoint robustness is also not deployment robustness: lighting, object appearance and surface materials are outside its scope.

### Industry implications

The paper attacks the economics of "deploy means re-collect". A bumped camera, a moved cell or a line changeover currently implies re-recording hundreds of demonstrations or redoing hand-eye calibration; a training-free adapter can turn that interruption into minutes of recovery.

For integrators, it demotes camera mounting precision from a hard process requirement to a variable that software can compensate, simplifying installation and maintenance. For VLA vendors, it is a necessary component for converting demo-day success rates into customer-site success rates — no customer will accept an instruction not to touch the camera.

The caution is that it covers one variable in the observation chain. A shippable deployment stack still has to handle lighting changes, substituted objects and human interference; view canonicalization is necessary but not sufficient.
