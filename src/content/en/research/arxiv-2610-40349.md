The data problem in dexterous manipulation has always been a cost problem rather than a theoretical one. Human demonstrations are rich enough. The difficulty is the transfer: human hands differ from robot hands in size, joint count, and skin friction, so a video or motion-capture trace of a person cannot be replayed as robot commands. The industry default is teleoperation — an operator holds a leader device and demonstrates over and over, slowly and tiringly, and one leader device usually serves one follower.

TouchBridge reframes that conversion as contact alignment rather than kinematic retargeting.

### Core innovation

**Hardware correspondence comes first.** The system consists of a wearable human demonstration interface and a corresponding robot interface, establishing cross-embodiment correspondence in visual and fingertip tactile sensing. This matters: most cross-embodiment pipelines map joint angles or end-effector poses, but what a contact-rich task actually requires is alignment of which patch of skin presses where, and how hard.

**Tac-Retarget: tactile-conditioned retargeting.** The method uses measured contact to align fingertip contact patches and preserves their spatial relations within a hand and across hands. It requires no object mesh, no object pose estimate, and no physics simulation. Those three dependencies are exactly what comparable methods assume, and exactly what breaks them in cluttered real scenes: object models are unavailable, pose estimates are wrong, and simulated contact parameters do not match reality.

**Visual synthesis and camera-anchored action conversion.** Tactile solves how contact happens; visual synthesis makes the observation look like a robot camera; camera-anchored conversion places the human motion in the robot camera frame. Together the three produce robot-domain demonstrations that can be used to fine-tune a VLA directly.

**A path into a pretrained VLA.** Converted data fine-tunes a pretrained vision-language-action model rather than training a policy from scratch.

### Results

On four real-world contact-rich tasks, fine-tuned only on TouchBridge-converted human demonstrations:

- **72.5%** average success, against **77.5%** for an equal number of teleoperated robot demonstrations — a five-point gap in exchange for a large gain in collection throughput.
- Human demonstration collection runs at **7.0x** the speed of teleoperation.
- Under the same policy and training setup, Tac-Retarget beats DexPilot by **25.0 percentage points** and UniDex by **42.5 points**.

That last comparison is the paper's most persuasive element. It shows the gain is not from "using human data" in some general sense but from the specific mechanism of tactile-conditioned contact-patch alignment: same data source, same policy, same training recipe, and swapping only the retargeting method moves results by twenty to forty points.

The 72.5% versus 77.5% pair deserves equal attention. Converted human demonstrations still trail real teleoperation. But 7x faster collection means the same labor hours buy seven times the task and scene diversity, and in dexterous manipulation diversity often determines generalization more than per-task volume does.

### Limitations

Only the average success over four tasks is reported; the abstract gives no per-task breakdown and does not describe the task set or objects, so it is impossible to tell whether 72.5% is dragged down by one hard task or is uniform. How many tactile sensors the wearable interface carries, where they sit, and whether they map one-to-one onto the robot hand is not detailed — and the fidelity of that cross-embodiment tactile correspondence sets the method's ceiling. Every experiment also assumes a demonstrator willing to wear the device; long-session comfort and interference with natural manipulation are not quantified, and the 7x figure covers collection only, excluding donning and calibration time. Finally, whether the five-point gap is statistically significant across four tasks is not stated, and no variance or confidence intervals are given.

### Industry implications

If the approach holds up on more tasks, it changes the cost structure of dexterous data collection. A teleoperation cell is a capital asset: leader device, workstation, calibration, trained operators, all serving one robot. A wearable interface is not — it can be issued to several demonstrators in parallel and the data pooled into one training pipeline. For humanoid teams that need to cover a long tail of tasks quickly, that is the difference between building a line and shipping devices.

The second implication is more technical. Not needing an object mesh or pose estimate means the conversion can work on objects never seen before. Industrial settings are full of parts with no CAD model, and household settings contain unbounded object variety; a conversion pipeline independent of object models is the one that can scale.

The third lesson is methodological. Comparing "converted human data" against "an equal amount of teleoperated data" under the same policy and training setup is a far cleaner experiment than reporting absolute success rates, because it isolates the value of the data pipeline itself. That control is worth adopting as standard practice for cross-embodiment data work.
