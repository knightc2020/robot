General-purpose manipulation has advanced almost entirely on the back of vision-language models: large-scale image-text pretraining supplied commonsense, object semantics, and instruction following, giving policies a first real shot at not being task-specific. In real manipulation that capability line hits a very concrete wall — semantics arrive, spatial precision does not. Knowing which cup to grasp is not the same as knowing where the handle sits in 3D, or how far the gripper can descend before contact. Vision-language backbones are broadly insensitive to those quantities, and industrial tasks are precisely where tolerances are tightest.

### Background

Several routes already exist for adding geometry to a VLA, each with a cost. Requiring explicit depth or point-cloud inputs pushes the burden onto sensors that may not be dependable on site. Compressing geometry into training-time supervision means geometry is no longer callable at inference, so performance degrades under new viewpoints or occlusion. Injecting geometry only at the model input or the action expert assumes geometric information needs to appear only at the two ends of the chain, leaving the intermediate vision backbone without persistent spatial context. All three share one flaw: none lets the backbone access geometry on demand, and cheaply, during inference.

### Core Innovation

StrataVLA turns geometric grounding from a one-shot injection into a layered, on-demand, cacheable mechanism built from three parts:

- **A frozen geometry foundation model.** It extracts geometric features directly from RGB observations, with no depth or point-cloud input and no training — avoiding yet another large model in need of fine-tuning.
- **Layer-specific Geometry Adapters.** Sparse adapters are attached only at selected backbone depths, letting those layers' visual representations retrieve task-relevant geometric evidence through cross-attention. Geometry is therefore distributed by depth rather than concentrated at one point.
- **Task-aware routing and an LRU feature cache.** These exploit temporal redundancy: geometry needs overlap heavily across adjacent timesteps, so features are reused and the geometry model is invoked only when genuinely required.

Together these make the method a plugin for an existing VLA rather than a change to its training paradigm.

### Results

- Across **LIBERO** suites, StrataVLA reaches **98.53% average success**, consistently ahead of strong VLA baselines.
- Stable gains are also observed on **SimplerEnv** and on **real-world manipulation** tasks.
- Task-aware routing and caching cut **geometry-model invocations by up to 88%**, converting geometric grounding from a compute burden into a controllable cost.

### Limitations

The abstract omits per-baseline numbers, the identity and size of the geometry foundation model, and cache hit rates, and it does not specify the platform or task count behind the real-robot experiments, so it is unclear whether the 88% saving holds across different task distributions. Caching rests on temporal redundancy, which can break down in fast-changing or heavily occluded scenes — exactly where spatial awareness is most needed. The frozen geometry model is still an added component in the inference chain, and its failure modes (for example, wrong geometry on reflective, transparent, or texture-poor surfaces) propagate straight into the policy. There is also no controlled comparison against depth-input approaches under matched conditions, and no account of how much training must change, leaving the boundary of "plug-and-play" unclear.

### Industry Implications

Two things block VLA adoption in industrial manipulation: insufficient spatial precision and inference cost that is too high. StrataVLA touches both — layered grounding for the former, routing and caching for the latter. An 88% reduction in invocations is a meaningful number because it decides whether the control loop fits a production real-time budget. More interesting is the no-retraining property: if geometric competence can be attached to an existing model like an adapter, then already-trained VLA assets have an upgrade path that does not require starting over. What production sites will actually ask about is a different set of questions — how reliable the geometry model is on awkward surfaces such as reflective metal or clear packaging, and how the system degrades when the cache stops helping. Leaderboards cannot answer those; only field validation can.
